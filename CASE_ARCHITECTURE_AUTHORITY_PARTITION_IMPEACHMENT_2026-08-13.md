# Case Architecture — Authority, Partition, Impeachment and Knowledge Asymmetry

**Date:** 13 August 2026  
**Status:** permanent analytical control note capturing the current case architecture developed during the 13 August session. This is not a primary source, pleading, expert report or finding of misconduct. Every proposition must remain traceable to its underlying source.

---

## 1. The governing case mechanism is now narrower and stronger

The case should no longer be framed primarily as a generic failure to reassess.

The current architecture is:

> **A mixed foot/movement presentation entered Neurology unresolved. A senior-linked treatment decision withheld levodopa, the feet were operationally routed to Podiatry while their neurological relationship remained `Not clear`, the patient says he protested that unresolved disposition before discharge, and the resulting architecture hardened before the patient left hospital. Later evidence repeatedly made the feet less `Not clear`, but was processed locally without being allowed to reopen the whole 2017 decision architecture.**

The later chronology therefore does not create the lock. It demonstrates the consequences of a lock already established in July 2017.

The critical distinction is:

> **The record permits local updating while resisting global updating.**

Later clinicians can acknowledge a parkinsonian foot throw, OFF dystonia, medication-responsive pain, Stalevo benefit, Sinemet benefit and profound Sinemet CR relief without ever producing the missing historical statement:

> **We were uncertain in 2017; we now know more; therefore we must revisit what we did with the feet in 2017.**

That missing act is central.

## 2. The July 2017 architecture

The case entered Neurology with the feet attached.

The originating presentation contained:

- longstanding disabling foot pain and structural foot pathology;
- corns/calluses and deformity;
- abnormal gait;
- toe clawing;
- markedly increased tone;
- movement-initiation difficulty;
- severe young-onset parkinsonism;
- dystonic-looking lower-limb phenomenology;
- an open atypical/genetic differential including PRKN/PARK2.

Neurology did not establish that the feet were neurologically separate. Its own conclusion was expressly:

> **`Not clear`.**

At the same time:

- conventional dopaminergic treatment was deliberately deferred;
- the treatment caution was linked to Prof Counihan and Neurology colleagues;
- Podiatry was actively operationalised;
- the GP on 6 July recorded the case being prioritised by Podiatry **`as Parkinson's`**;
- the discharge plan recorded that the hospital SHO had tried to arrange Podiatry;
- the discharge narrative nevertheless retained `Not clear` as the clinical relationship state.

Thus the documentary state was uncertainty while the operational state was separation.

The key formulation is:

> **The documentary state remained `Not clear`; the operative state was separation.**

## 3. Why the no-levodopa decision is structurally central

The treatment policy was not merely an isolated drug choice. It was part of the architecture that allowed the foot/movement relationship to remain suspended.

The contemporaneous Monaghan-to-Healy correspondence states:

> **`Myself and Tim are keen to hold back on Levodopa insofar as maybe possible unless you'd advise otherwise?`**

The reproduced 2017 record and later Monaghan material identify Prof Timothy Counihan as the senior local neurologist linked to exceptional care/caution with future levodopa/dopamine-agonist treatment.

The patient-source account additionally preserves the recollection that the treatment position was communicated as a senior decision — including the phrase that `my boss told me not to give you the good stuff`. That remains patient-source evidence unless independently corroborated and must not be silently converted into documentary fact.

The analytical importance of the senior treatment constraint is this:

> **If levodopa remains outside the immediate treatment architecture, the movement disorder can be managed conservatively while the unresolved feet can be routed elsewhere. The `Not clear` relationship need not be resolved before discharge.**

This does not itself establish that levodopa should have been used diagnostically or therapeutically in July 2017. That remains an expert question. But the treatment constraint forms part of the architecture within which unresolved foot causation was allowed to persist.

## 4. Counihan, Healy and Monaghan — the three-position comparison

This comparison is now central and must remain source-exact.

### A. Galway / Counihan-linked position before the second opinion

Monaghan asks Prof Daniel Healy for genetic advice and a possible second opinion and writes:

> **`Myself and Tim are keen to hold back on Levodopa insofar as maybe possible unless you'd advise otherwise?`**

This directly establishes:

- a continuing Galway position favouring levodopa deferral;
- that the position was not represented as Monaghan's alone;
- that Healy was expressly invited to advise otherwise.

The contemporaneous page itself does not identify `Tim`; the separate 2025 Monaghan account identifies Counihan as the senior neurologist whose advice had informed treatment caution.

### B. Healy's 12 September 2017 second opinion

After personally seeing the patient, Healy writes:

> **`I think probably has Park II and I would suggest testing that initially.`**

He records dramatic improvement in motor symptoms on low-dose Procyclidine and Rasagiline.

He further writes:

> **`patients with PARK II tend to manage quite well for long periods of time with relatively low doses of Dopamine replacement therapy. They can become quite dyskinetic if over-used.`**

Healy does not order immediate levodopa. His actual position is therefore not `start high-dose levodopa now`.

But neither is it simply the Galway formulation of prolonged non-use. His wording describes PARK II management in terms of **relatively low dopamine replacement with caution against over-use**.

This creates a real distinction between:

> **cautious low-dose replacement**

and

> **continued avoidance for as long as possible / for quite some time.**

### C. Monaghan after receiving Healy's opinion

The 24 April 2018 note is unusually important because it places several propositions together in the same evidential setting.

Monaghan records the prior Procyclidine withdrawal history, including that the first thing changing rapidly was the outside-left-foot/corn-site phenomenon and that the patient became more `shifty` and `unrelaxed` off Procyclidine.

He then writes:

> **`Obviously he could be very much better if we would actually give him the levodopa or another dopamine agonist ---however for a very clearly set out reasons, we do not want to do that – at least for quite some time.`**

And in the genetics section he writes:

> **`It doesn’t particularly change current management – so there is no great loss in “not knowing” currently right now.`**

while describing:

> **`the Specific PARK 2 PRKN which we/Prof Healy suspect is the cause`**

and acknowledging that isolated PARK2/PRKN testing or a panel could reasonably be done if the patient wished.

This is a central architectural fact.

By April 2018:

- Healy's second opinion had strengthened PRKN from possibility to `probably has Park II`;
- Healy had recommended testing PARK II first;
- Healy had described PARK II in terms of long-term relatively low dopamine replacement with caution against over-use;
- the Procyclidine withdrawal history had supplied neurological pharmacological modifiability within the disputed foot/lower-limb domain;
- Monaghan expressly recognised that levodopa could make the patient `very much better`;
- yet the operative treatment position remained `we do not want to do that – at least for quite some time`;
- and the value of confirming the suspected cause was expressly discounted because it `doesn't particularly change current management`.

The comparison therefore raises the following control question:

> **Did the pre-existing Galway treatment plan govern the interpretation of Healy's opinion, rather than Healy's opinion being allowed to reopen the treatment plan?**

That question is stronger than saying Healy `ordered levodopa` and was ignored. He did not. The issue is whether the second opinion altered the governing architecture at all.

The April 2018 note suggests that the suspected diagnosis and the treatment plan were being processed in opposite directions:

> **the evidence moved toward PARK2 while management was declared substantially unchanged by knowing PARK2.**

This is a major path-dependence signal.

## 5. `Not clear` had two possible futures

`Not clear` was not a final diagnosis. It was an unresolved branch point.

It had two legitimate possible futures.

### Future A — separation becomes more strongly established

Later evidence could have shown that the painful foot syndrome was substantially local, mechanical or peripheral and independent of the movement disorder.

### Future B — integration becomes more strongly established

Later evidence could have shown that at least part of the foot/lower-limb syndrome was neurologically medication-sensitive, dystonic, OFF-related or otherwise part of the movement disorder.

The longitudinal record increasingly supplied Future-B evidence while never eliminating the real local pathology.

The case therefore does not require `all foot pain was Parkinsonian`.

The stronger proposition is:

> **The original uncertainty moved progressively toward a mixed but materially neurological model. The historical management partition did not visibly move with it.**

## 6. The Procyclidine hinge

The Procyclidine chronology must remain exact.

The discharge plan contemplated slow titration and an optional later test stop if there had been no obvious benefit. The actual later withdrawal occurred because of visual blurring and lasted approximately two weeks. Those events must not be falsely conflated.

But the reason for the actual withdrawal does not erase the information it generated.

The evidence converges as follows:

- contemporaneous patient email during withdrawal: tension rebuilding, feet restless, walk falling apart;
- later October 2017 patient email: medication stop, lower-dose restart, incomplete control, re-escalation, dramatic global physical improvement described as `living in a miracle`;
- 24 April 2018 treating-neurologist note: the first thing changing rapidly was the outside-left-foot/corn-site phenomenon, symptoms worsened, and the patient was more `shifty` and `unrelaxed` off Procyclidine.

The safe conclusion is:

> **At least part of the disputed foot/lower-limb domain was neurologically pharmacologically modifiable by late 2017 and was personally known to the treating neurologist no later than 24 April 2018.**

This did not prove that the corn itself was neurological, every foot symptom was dystonia, or levodopa would necessarily have produced the same effect.

Its significance is architectural:

> **The Procyclidine event made the original foot/movement relationship less `Not clear`.**

A proper response therefore required the original architecture to become eligible for reopening.

## 7. What integration actually required

Integration never meant declaring every foot symptom Parkinsonian.

It meant reopening the **whole decision architecture built on the original partition**.

A proper reintegration exercise would have required reconsideration of:

1. the original `Not clear` finding;
2. the scope and purpose of the Podiatry allocation;
3. a component-level symptom map distinguishing corn/pressure pain, burning, toe clawing/curling, abnormal posture/throw, restlessness, gait deterioration, OFF/morning pain and central pain modulation;
4. the exact meaning of the Procyclidine withdrawal/restoration response;
5. whether peripheral neuropathy remained merely a hypothesis or had acquired objective support;
6. the limited meaning of local-treatment benefit from supports, paring or bunion covers;
7. the limited meaning of pregabalin response and the importance of failure/incomplete control at high dose;
8. the accumulating dystonic/PRKN/parkinsonian lower-limb phenotype;
9. the continuing levodopa-deferral risk/benefit balance;
10. whether ordinary longitudinal dopaminergic treatment response could supply phenotyping information and what the patient was told about that;
11. the meaning of later Sinemet responsiveness;
12. the patient's actual position, distinct from clinician-recorded `good understanding`;
13. psychological and functional severity;
14. specialty ownership and feedback between Podiatry and Neurology;
15. the PRKN diagnostic branch and whether etiological clarification was truly management-neutral;
16. the longitudinal record itself — requiring one explicit reconciliation rather than isolated local explanations.

The governing proposition is:

> **Integration required more than acknowledging that Parkinson medication helped. It required allowing that fact to reopen the Podiatry allocation, peripheral-neuropathy hypothesis, levodopa-risk balance, symptom classification, patient understanding and historical interpretation of the case.**

Without that broader reconsideration, neurological evidence could be incorporated therapeutically without being integrated diagnostically or institutionally.

## 8. The protest may be the actual foreclosure event

The patient reports that shortly before discharge, knowing discharge was imminent, he asked medical students to summon the neurologist and protested that the feet had not been dealt with, using the analogy of receiving a full NCT and being sent out on bald tyres.

He recalls the neurologist responding in substance that it was as easy for the patient to obtain a Podiatry appointment as for the neurologist.

This remains patient-source evidence pending independent corroboration.

But its analytical significance must be understood correctly.

If the protest occurred, the patient did not wait until 2018 or 2025 to demand reopening.

He had already put the unresolved foot problem directly back before Neurology **before leaving hospital**.

If the answer was Podiatry rather than reassessment, then the potential foreclosure sequence is:

> **unresolved mixed presentation → senior-linked levodopa deferral → feet operationally partitioned to Podiatry → patient protests unresolved feet → no documented reopening → protest omitted from final narrative → discharge architecture hardens.**

The later red flags would therefore not be first opportunities to reconsider. They would be repeated confirmations of an opportunity already presented and declined at discharge.

The omission matters because the durable discharge record then contains:

- `Not clear`;
- Podiatry;
- treatment deferral;
- extensive patient-understanding language;

but not the reported direct challenge to the adequacy of that arrangement.

If corroborated, this would materially impeach the discharge narrative as a complete account of patient disagreement and would destroy any inference that silence proves consensual closure.

The broader case does not depend entirely on proving the protest. The Procyclidine and later longitudinal evidence independently challenge the partition.

## 9. Patient and chart are competing primary sources on different propositions

This is now a permanent evidential rule:

> **The patient and chart should be treated as competing primary evidential sources on different propositions, not as a primary chart and subordinate allegation.**

The patient is primary for:

- pain;
- symptom location and quality;
- subjective severity;
- bodily medication effects;
- treatment preferences;
- requests;
- protest/conversations experienced by him;
- functional experience;
- lived chronology.

The chart is primary or stronger for:

- objective test results;
- recorded examination findings;
- prescriptions;
- institutional decisions;
- clinician interpretations;
- what clinicians themselves wrote or communicated.

Neither source has categorical priority across all domains.

This rule is essential because the chart has now been materially impeached as a self-sufficient longitudinal narrative on disputed patient-position, causal-classification and reassessment propositions.

A clinician-authored statement that the patient `understood`, `accepted`, `did not want`, or `agreed` proves first that the clinician recorded that proposition. It does not automatically prove the underlying patient state.

## 10. Knowledge asymmetry and authority asymmetry

The relevant asymmetry is not ordinary `doctor knows medicine / patient does not` asymmetry.

The case contains multiple distinct asymmetries:

1. **technical knowledge asymmetry** — clinicians understand neurology, differentials, tests and treatment risks;
2. **decision-state asymmetry** — clinicians know what remains uncertain, what has been deferred and what institutional alternatives exist;
3. **causal-meaning asymmetry** — the patient experiences medication effects while clinicians control their formal interpretation;
4. **record asymmetry** — the institution authors the durable account inherited by later actors;
5. **authority asymmetry** — only the institution can make information govern tests, referrals, prescriptions, diagnosis and discharge;
6. **retrospective-access asymmetry** — the patient later has to reconstruct decisions from a record authored by those who possessed more institutional knowledge contemporaneously.

The governing sentence is:

> **The patient supplies the signal. The institution controls whether the signal becomes knowledge.**

The asymmetry operates reciprocally:

- the patient knows what the feet feel like and what changes when medication changes;
- clinicians know what that observation could mean diagnostically and what action it can trigger;
- but only the clinical institution can convert the observation into an official change in the model.

This gives the patient's `bald tyres` protest a deeper meaning. It may represent the maximum challenge available from his information state: `the thing that brought me here remains unresolved and you are discharging me anyway`.

The institution possessed the technical vocabulary and authority needed to translate that signal into the causal and governance question.

## 11. Treatment understanding is not diagnostic-state understanding

The 2017 discharge record strongly supports extensive explanation of the therapeutic reasons for levodopa/dopamine-agonist deferral.

It records `good understanding` of why those treatments were being delayed.

But that cannot safely be expanded into proof that the patient understood:

- every unresolved diagnostic question;
- what clinicians themselves believed response to dopaminergic treatment could reveal;
- any diagnostic/phenotyping information postponed by deferral;
- or the full consequences of allowing the foot question to remain unresolved while Podiatry became operational.

The safe formulation is:

> **The patient is documented as understanding why treatment was delayed; the record does not establish that he and the institution understood the same decision in all its therapeutic and diagnostic dimensions.**

No allegation of intentional withholding is presently established.

The relevant expert question is:

> **What did this team contemporaneously believe ordinary dopaminergic response could tell them about phenotype or diagnosis, and what of that significance was communicated to the patient?**

> **Was that interpretation a sincere, independently supported mixed-mechanism judgment, or did it function as a self-protective reinterpretation that neutralised evidence threatening the defensibility of the original partition and treatment architecture?**

Awareness is much more strongly established than improper motive.

## 13. How to test knowing frame preservation without assuming dishonesty

Do not begin with the label `dishonesty`.

Build the knowledge sequence.

For every key event ask:

1. What did Monaghan personally know?
2. When did he know it?
3. What previous proposition did the new information threaten?
4. What did he record?
5. What did he omit or leave unreconciled?
6. What did he do next?
7. Did the new evidence change the governing architecture, or was it given a local meaning that preserved that architecture?
8. Did the explanatory model exist contemporaneously, or appear principally once earlier management was challenged?
9. What independent evidence established the retained peripheral generator?
10. What observation could have falsified the `pain of whatever cause` / modulation explanation?

The harder inference of conscious frame preservation strengthens if:

- peripheral neuropathy remained conditional rather than objectively established;
- Podiatry did not resolve the patient's problem;
- pregabalin failed to provide durable control despite high dose;
- neurological/OFF/dystonic evidence accumulated;
- the original Procyclidine response was never historically reconciled;
- the fully articulated modulation theory appears chiefly in retrospective defence;
- and no falsification condition for the retained peripheral model is identifiable.

The good-faith explanation strengthens if contemporaneous records and expert evidence show a consistently held, component-specific mixed-mechanism model with positive independent support and rational treatment thresholds.

## 14. The record itself is now part of the case mechanism

The record should no longer be treated simply as a neutral description of the patient's condition.

The comparative audits established that substantially the same clinical events generated materially different global conclusions depending on whether clinician-authored narrative received presumptive interpretive authority.

The present classification is therefore:

> **Reliable in many direct factual components; materially impeached as a self-sufficient longitudinal narrative; unsafe for unqualified reliance on disputed causal, patient-position and adequacy-of-reassessment propositions.**

The chart may accurately record an observation while still fail as a fair account of what that observation should have done to the governing model.

That distinction is essential.

The record can prove that a clinician later discussed OFF dystonia, neuropathic pain, medication response or patient preference.

It cannot automatically prove:

- that the original partition was adequately reconsidered;
- that the patient agreed with it;
- that neuropathy was established;
- that Podiatry resolved the problem;
- that later explanations reproduce contemporaneous reasoning;
- or that disagreement did not occur merely because it is absent.

## 18. Writing and reasoning rules from this point

1. Do not let the chart authenticate its own completeness.
2. Do not treat clinician-recorded patient preference as independent patient evidence.
3. Do not convert `Not clear`, `perhaps`, `may`, `if` or `neuropathic` into established causal facts.
4. Do not require the patient to argue his way out of the chart before assessing the chart's reliability.
5. Keep the patient's voice as a primary evidential layer.
6. Preserve genuine structural foot disease and mixed causation; do not overclaim that all pain was dystonia.
7. Preserve the exact distinction between Healy's low-dose dopamine-replacement model and Galway's continuing deferral; do not falsely say Healy ordered immediate levodopa.
8. Preserve the exact distinction between Counihan's presently reconstructed role and what the missing native Complex Case Meeting record may eventually show.
9. Treat April 2018 as a major documentary hinge but remember that, if corroborated, the patient's discharge protest may show the reopening demand was already made before discharge.
10. Test state of mind through cumulative knowledge, interpretation and action, not through rhetoric.
11. Ask repeatedly: **what evidence would have been allowed to make `Not clear` more clear, and what would have happened to the 2017 architecture if it did?**
12. End materially significant sessions with a new or updated present-outlook/control note so the repository does not lag behind the analysis.
