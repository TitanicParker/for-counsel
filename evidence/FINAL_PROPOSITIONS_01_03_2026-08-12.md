# Final Propositions 01–03 — 12 August 2026

**Status:** finalized working propositions for counsel/expert development. These are not pleaded conclusions. Each proposition preserves documentary fact, contemporaneous patient evidence, later patient recollection, inference, and expert-dependent judgment as separate layers.

---

## Proposition 1 — The July 2017 dopaminergic-deferral rule became controlling without a documented reopening mechanism

> **In July 2017, Neurology adopted a deliberate policy of deferring levodopa/dopamine agonists for `as long as possible`. The policy was formed while the diagnosis and lower-limb phenotype were still evolving, and the surviving record does not identify what deterioration, treatment response, specialist opinion, diagnostic development or passage of time would require that decision to be reconsidered.**

### Supporting facts

1. Severe Parkinsonism was already recognised during the first admission, with marked rigidity, gross bradykinesia, abnormal gait and major functional impairment.
2. The phenotype was atypical for straightforward idiopathic Parkinson's disease: very young probable onset, marked symmetry, dystonic-looking gait/lower-limb posture and other atypical features were documented.
3. PRKN/PARK2 was already a live etiological possibility.
4. The decision not to use conventional dopaminergic treatment was deliberate. The contemporaneous treatment note records an intention to avoid levodopa/dopamine agonists `for as long as possible`.
5. The treatment caution was associated in the record with Prof Counihan and Neurology colleagues. Monaghan later wrote to Healy: `Myself and Tim are keen to hold back on Levodopa insofar as maybe possible unless you'd advise otherwise?`
6. Monaghan simultaneously contemplated substantial benefit and possible future regret from delay.
7. No presently identified source defines a disability threshold, review date, failed-treatment threshold, medication-response trigger, specialist-opinion trigger or other stopping rule for ending `as long as possible`.
8. Healy subsequently strengthened the etiological hypothesis to `probably has Park II` and described patients with PARK2 as often managing for long periods on relatively low-dose dopamine replacement while warning against over-use/dyskinesia.
9. By April 2018 Galway still recorded PARK2/PRKN as the suspected cause and acknowledged that Liam could `obviously ... be very much better` with dopaminergic treatment, while maintaining the position that `we do not want to do that - at least for quite some time`.
10. The same 2018 note characterised confirmation of suspected PARK2 as not particularly changing `current management`.
11. Total levodopa deferral eventually ended in 2021, proving that `as long as possible` had an endpoint in practice. The documentary question is what made that endpoint arrive and why earlier developments did not.

### Patient voice

Liam recalls that within approximately one hour of the Complex Case Meeting, Monaghan returned appearing notably unhappy/forlorn and said:

> `My boss told me not to give you the good stuff, and in time I'm afraid we might come to regret it.`

Liam recalls asking why and says Monaghan explained, colloquially, the uncertainty of proving Parkinson's disease during life and the existence of treatments that had worked in people with the disease. Liam does not recall any explanation of the diagnostic value of a levodopa challenge or any defined condition for reconsidering the decision.

He further recalls Monaghan saying, in substance:

> `I just don't want you coming back in seven or eight years' time saying my life could have been better.`

Liam's contemporaneous understanding was simple: Monaghan was not solely calling the shots and the treatment decision had been made above or outside Monaghan's sole discretion.

### Source-status boundary

- The recalled bedside conversation is patient-source evidence unless independently corroborated.
- Liam can directly evidence how the decision was communicated to and understood by him.
- His observation that Monaghan appeared forlorn/unhappy is evidence of demeanour; Monaghan's private state of mind remains inferential.
- This proposition does **not** assert that levodopa necessarily had to be started in July 2017 or that caution in a very young patient was irrational.

### Expert question

> **Was it clinically reasonable for an expressly open-ended dopaminergic-deferral strategy to remain in force without a defined review mechanism as the evidential state changed toward probable PARK2 and substantial treatment benefit remained expressly contemplated?**

## Proposition 2 — Neurology's own treatment functionally demonstrated that the GP-referred gait–feet–tension complex could move together under neurological medication

> **Within months of the July 2017 admission, Neurology's procyclidine/Kemadrin treatment produced marked improvement across the same cluster that had prompted the GP referral — gait, feet and muscle tension — and a planned withdrawal experiment was followed by deterioration that included the feet. Whatever the precise mechanism, Neurology's own treatment had functionally demonstrated that the referred symptom complex could move together under neurological medication.**

### Supporting facts

1. Before admission, the GP had linked Liam's gait, feet and `muscle tension` in the Neurology referral pathway.
2. During admission, Neurology documented genuine structural foot pathology, a dystonic-looking lower-limb abnormality and an unresolved relationship between the feet and neurological syndrome: `Not clear`.
3. Podiatry nevertheless became the operative pathway for the feet while Neurology treated the Parkinsonism.
4. Procyclidine/Kemadrin was prescribed by Neurology and became, in practical terms, the first pharmacological answer to the neurological referral complex.
5. A contemporaneous 4 October 2017 email records Monaghan's staged Kemadrin titration and a planned treatment break so Liam could assess what effects the medication was having.
6. Liam wrote contemporaneously that within days there had been significant improvement and that he had been `living in a miracle, literally`.
7. He listed concrete functional restoration: `My walk is back together`, writing improved, he could stand straight, his legs were `loose and limber`, and multiple ordinary movement/dexterity functions returned.
8. Four days into the treatment break, Liam wrote: `I am feeling the tension building up, my feet are restless and my walk is falling apart....but my eyesight is back to normal.`
9. A separate 24 October 2017 HR email records that the Parkinson's nurse had withdrawn the medication for about 12 days because of visual problems, reintroduced it at a lower dose, and then advised re-escalation because the lower dose did not control all symptoms.
10. In that HR email Liam wrote that, apart from the visual problem, he was `physically very much improved` and that normal movement/function was `the equivalent of living in a miracle`.
11. Those emails were written contemporaneously for family/workplace purposes, not for complaint or litigation.
12. Liam now recalls that the procyclidine effect felt like a profound release of longstanding bodily tension and restoration of powerful near-normal walking.
13. He recalls that when procyclidine was withdrawn, the **first focal pain to return** was the pain on the outside of the left foot, the same persistent pain site he dates back to 2004.
14. The outside-left-foot pain may have had a mechanical component from recurrent loading/corn formation. A neurological treatment could therefore reduce that pain by altering lower-limb tone, posture or loading without the corn itself being `neurological`.
15. The surviving record does not presently show a reasoned neurological account of what the medication-foot interaction meant for the earlier Podiatry/Neurology partition or for the levodopa-deferral rule.

### Patient voice and trust

Liam says the success of the 2017 treatment consolidated his trust in Neurology. He believed he was in good hands, liked the clinicians involved, and says that trust persisted until the morning of 14 March 2025.

That matters because the absence of repeated protest during the intervening years should not automatically be read as informed agreement with every etiological or treatment assumption. His operating position was trust.

### Source-status boundary

- The October 2017 emails are contemporaneous patient-origin evidence.
- The claim that the outside-left-foot pain was the *first* focal pain to return is present patient recollection unless independently corroborated.
- The proposition does **not** assert that procyclidine proved the feet were dystonia.
- It does **not** assert that procyclidine and levodopa are pharmacologically interchangeable.
- It asserts the narrower, observable point that Neurology's treatment altered the same symptom complex that had been referred to it.

### Expert questions

> **What clinical significance should a treating neurologist attach to a planned withdrawal of antiparkinsonian medication followed by deterioration that includes the feet, where the neurological relationship of the feet had previously been left unresolved?**

> **Could altered lower-limb tone/posture plausibly reduce focal mechanically generated foot pain by changing loading, even where the painful corn or callus itself is structurally real?**

## Proposition 3 — The feet were not merely omitted; their operational separation was repeatedly preserved despite stronger treatment-response evidence than the competing neuropathy/alcohol hypothesis

> **From 2017 onward, the feet continued to be allocated to alternative explanatory pathways — initially Podiatry/structural pathology and later peripheral neuropathy/alcohol — while neurological medication repeatedly altered the feet. By 2025, peripheral neuropathy remained expressly conditional and the hoped-for benefit of alcohol cessation remained speculative, whereas dopaminergic relief of the feet was directly observed and further benefit from increased Sinemet was expected by the neurologist. The surviving record therefore supports testing whether the feet were merely overlooked or whether the clinical architecture repeatedly preserved their separation from the Parkinsonian treatment model.**

### Supporting facts

1. Peripheral neuropathy entered the case early as a possible explanation for painful feet before the full movement disorder had been understood. Its early appearance was not inherently unreasonable.
2. Once severe Parkinsonism was recognised, Monaghan was also keen to separate alcohol from the cause of the principal neurological condition. Liam recalls carrying concern that he might somehow have caused his illness through drinking.
3. Neuropathy nevertheless survived as a possible explanation for the feet while the feet continued to be managed outside the core Parkinsonian model.
4. From 2023 onward, severe foot pain was repeatedly considered alongside peripheral neuropathy and alcohol use.
5. In 2024 Neurology expressly recorded concern about `alcohol related neuropathy`.
6. The surviving record presently does not establish that peripheral neuropathy was objectively demonstrated as the cause of the disabling foot pain, nor that alcohol was objectively established as its cause.
7. By 2025 Liam's morning foot pain was extreme: the first steps were described as `torture`, he could not comfortably tie his runners and the feet dominated the consultation.
8. Yet the neurologist still wrote conditionally: `If there is a degree of peripheral neuropathy here, the alcohol being gone will hopefully slow the deterioration of that.`
9. In the same clinical period the neurologist documented a much less conditional observation: `Once the Parkinsons medications hit his system he does have a relief in the feelings in the feet.`
10. He also wrote: `He knows very well that once the Sinemet hits his system that the feet feel better from a pain perspective.`
11. And further: `I have little doubt other than that if we increased the Sinemet, he would probably get some further relief from the pain.`
12. This creates an evidential asymmetry: neuropathy remained `if`; alcohol cessation remained `hopefully`; the dopaminergic effect was observed and further dopaminergic benefit was expected.
13. In the later March 2025 rebuttal, written in response to criticism of anchoring/interpretive bias, the language moved from the clinic note's conditional `if there is a degree of peripheral neuropathy` to a more settled formulation of `peripheral neuropathic pain symptomatologies`, while simultaneously acknowledging that Sinemet reduced the pain and that Sinemet CR had produced profound relief.
14. The presently identified record does not show an intervening objective diagnostic event explaining that increase in neuropathic certainty.
15. The defensive account therefore appears to preserve the feet outside the Parkinsonian cause by characterising Parkinson's as magnifying pain from another cause even while dopaminergic treatment is materially relieving it.

### Patient voice and communication effect

Liam says he never believed the longstanding foot pain was caused by alcohol. He distinguishes that from his earlier anxiety that his drinking might somehow have contributed to his broader physical condition.

He says that by 2023–2025 he had learned that raising severe foot pain in Neurology was liable to return the discussion to alcohol and neuropathy. He challenged Monaghan's opinions about alcohol and weight on several occasions, but increasingly experienced the feet as a subject that would not lead to meaningful neurological reconsideration.

His description is not that he became incapable of talking about the feet; he could and did report them to his GP. The narrower patient proposition is that he became worn down in his expectation that raising the feet within Neurology would produce a different explanation or useful action.

Liam also says he had already formed, at discharge in 2017, the understanding that Neurology did not intend to own the feet in the way he needed it to. He did not consciously rehearse the bedside protest for years; the memory resurfaced only on 7 July 2025 during his Article 15 GDPR work, when he recognised that four medical students had potentially witnessed the exchange.

### Source-status boundary

- The repeated documentary association of feet, neuropathy and alcohol is a record fact.
- Liam's expectation that mentioning his feet would trigger discussion of drinking is patient evidence about the communication effect of that clinical framing.
- His statement that he did not believe alcohol caused the foot pain is direct evidence of his own contemporaneous understanding.
- The proposition does **not** assert that neuropathy was impossible, that alcohol was irrelevant, or that every foot symptom was dystonia.
- It does **not** allege deliberate motive or bad faith.
- It asks whether an alternative explanation was allowed to remain operationally useful without being resolved to the same evidential standard as the directly observed medication-response relationship.

### Expert questions

> **Where peripheral neuropathy is repeatedly invoked as a possible explanation for disabling foot pain in a patient under longitudinal neurological care, what objective assessment would ordinarily be expected to establish whether neuropathy is present, characterize it and determine its likely cause?**

> **How should a neurologist reconcile a conditional neuropathy hypothesis — `if there is a degree` — and a hoped-for benefit from alcohol cessation with directly observed foot-pain relief after Sinemet and an expressed expectation that more Sinemet would probably give further relief?**

> **If the same clinician later describes the pain in more settled peripheral-neuropathic terms, what new evidence would ordinarily justify that increased diagnostic certainty?**

## Combined architecture of Propositions 1–3

The propositions fit together without requiring proof that all foot pain was dystonia or that levodopa necessarily had to be started in July 2017:

1. **A governing rule was created:** conventional dopaminergic treatment would be avoided `for as long as possible`, without a documented stopping/reopening rule.
2. **Neurology's own treatment generated contrary information almost immediately:** by October 2017, the GP-referred gait–feet–tension complex moved together under procyclidine/Kemadrin.
3. **The feet nevertheless remained outside the core disease model:** alternative structural/podiatric and later neuropathy/alcohol explanations preserved that separation while neurological medication repeatedly changed the feet.
4. **By 2025 the asymmetry was explicit:** neuropathy remained conditional and alcohol benefit hopeful, while dopaminergic foot-pain relief was observed and further benefit expected.

### Controlling forensic question

> **What evidence was capable of moving the feet back into the neurological disease model?**

If the original referral, dystonic-looking lower limbs, procyclidine response, withdrawal deterioration, later OFF/dystonic reasoning, Sinemet responsiveness and eventual profound benefit from altered dopaminergic coverage were all insufficient to trigger documented reclassification or formal reassessment, the issue is no longer well described as simple failure to integrate. It becomes appropriate to test whether the clinical architecture itself repeatedly preserved the separation.
