# Preliminary Propositions 01–02 — 12 August 2026

**Status:** first formal proposition set, still open to refinement before counsel-facing consolidation.

These propositions distinguish documentary fact, patient voice, inference and expert-dependent judgment.

---

## Proposition 1 — The July 2017 dopaminergic-deferral rule became controlling without a documented reopening mechanism

> **In July 2017, Neurology adopted a deliberate policy of deferring levodopa/dopamine agonists for `as long as possible`. The policy was formed while the diagnosis and lower-limb phenotype were still evolving, and the surviving record does not identify what clinical deterioration, treatment response, specialist opinion, diagnostic development or passage of time would require that decision to be reconsidered.**

### Supporting documentary facts

1. Severe Parkinsonism was already recognised during the first admission, with marked rigidity, gross bradykinesia, abnormal gait and major functional impairment.
2. The phenotype was not straightforward idiopathic PD: marked symmetry, very young probable onset, atypical features and dystonic-looking gait/lower-limb posture were documented.
3. PRKN/PARK2 was already a live etiological possibility in Galway.
4. The treatment decision was deliberate: the contemporaneous record records an intention to avoid levodopa/dopamine agonists `for as long as possible`.
5. The treatment caution was associated with Prof Counihan and Neurology colleagues, and Monaghan later wrote to Healy: `Myself and Tim are keen to hold back on Levodopa insofar as maybe possible unless you'd advise otherwise?`
6. Monaghan simultaneously contemplated substantial benefit and future regret from delay.
7. No presently identified source defines a disability threshold, review date, failed-treatment threshold, medication-response trigger, specialist-opinion trigger or other stopping rule for ending `as long as possible`.
8. Healy later strengthened the etiological hypothesis to `probably has Park II` and described long-term treatment with relatively low-dose dopamine replacement while warning against over-use.
9. By April 2018 Galway still recorded PARK2/PRKN as the suspected cause and acknowledged that Liam could `obviously ... be very much better` with dopaminergic treatment, but the policy remained to avoid it `for quite some time`.
10. The same 2018 note characterised confirmation of suspected PARK2 as not particularly changing `current management`.
11. Total levodopa deferral eventually ended in 2021, showing that `as long as possible` had an endpoint in practice; the documentary question is what made that endpoint arrive and why earlier developments did not.

### Patient voice

Liam recalls that within approximately one hour of the Complex Case Meeting, Monaghan returned appearing notably unhappy/forlorn and said:

> `My boss told me not to give you the good stuff, and in time I'm afraid we might come to regret it.`

Liam recalls asking why. He says Monaghan explained, in colloquial terms, the uncertainty of proving Parkinson's disease during life and the existence of treatments that had worked in people with the disease. Liam does not recall any explanation of the diagnostic value of a levodopa challenge.

Liam further recalls Monaghan saying, in substance:

> `I just don't want you coming back in seven or eight years' time saying my life could have been better.`

Liam's contemporaneous understanding was that Monaghan was not solely calling the shots and that the decision had been made above or outside Monaghan's sole discretion. He recalls no stated review date, disability threshold or other condition by which the decision would be reconsidered.

### Source-status boundary

- The quoted/recalled bedside words are patient-source evidence unless independently corroborated.
- Liam can directly evidence how he understood the decision to have been communicated.
- His impression that Monaghan was `forlorn` or unhappy is an observation of demeanour; any claim about Monaghan's private state of mind remains inferential.
- The proposition does **not** assert that levodopa necessarily had to be started in July 2017 or that the caution was irrational.

### Expert question

> **Was it clinically reasonable for an open-ended dopaminergic-deferral strategy to remain in force without a defined review mechanism, particularly as the evidential state changed toward probable PARK2 and substantial treatment benefit remained expressly contemplated?**

## Proposition 2 — Neurology's own treatment regime generated direct evidence that the feet were medication-responsive, but the significance of that signal was not visibly incorporated into the neurological treatment model

> **Within months of the July 2017 admission, Neurology's own procyclidine/Kemadrin treatment produced marked physical improvement, and a planned withdrawal experiment was followed by deterioration that included the feet. Whatever the mechanism, this was direct patient-specific evidence that the neurological treatment regime could materially alter the feet. The surviving record does not show that this finding caused Neurology to define what the response meant for the earlier Podiatry/Neurology partition or for future treatment decisions.**

### Supporting documentary and contemporaneous patient facts

1. Before admission, the GP had already linked gait, feet and `muscle tension` in the Neurology referral pathway.
2. During admission, Neurology itself documented a mixed picture: real structural foot pathology, a dystonic-looking lower-limb abnormality, and a relationship recorded as `Not clear`.
3. Podiatry nevertheless became the operative pathway for the feet while Neurology treated the Parkinsonism.
4. Procyclidine/Kemadrin was prescribed by Neurology as part of the neurological treatment regime.
5. In a 4 October 2017 contemporaneous email written during a planned Kemadrin break, Liam recorded that Monaghan had instructed a staged titration and a treatment break after several weeks so that Liam could assess what effects the drug was having.
6. The same email records that within days of beginning treatment there had been significant improvement and that Liam had been `living in a miracle, literally`.
7. Liam contemporaneously listed concrete functional gains: `My walk is back together`, writing improved, he could stand straight, his legs were `loose and limber`, and he could perform multiple ordinary movement and dexterity tasks that had previously been difficult.
8. Four days into the treatment break, he wrote: `I am feeling the tension building up, my feet are restless and my walk is falling apart....but my eyesight is back to normal.`
9. A 24 October 2017 HR email separately records that the Parkinson's nurse had taken Liam off the medication for about 12 days because of visual problems, then reintroduced it at a lower dose; the lower dose did not control all symptoms and he was advised to increase it again while monitoring vision.
10. In that same HR email Liam wrote: `apart from the vision, I am physically very much improved, you wouldn't believe there was ever much wrong with me` and described normal movement/function as `the equivalent of living in a miracle`.
11. These emails were contemporaneous and written for family/workplace purposes, not litigation or retrospective complaint.
12. The treatment-response sequence therefore established, at minimum, that neurological medication materially altered the patient's physical state and that withdrawal deterioration included the feet.
13. The surviving record does not presently show a reasoned neurological account of what that medication-foot interaction meant.
14. Nor does the record presently show that this signal caused the earlier Podiatry/Neurology allocation or the criteria for prolonged dopaminergic deferral to be formally reconsidered.
15. In October 2021, Sinemet entered the treatment architecture and the clinic record expressly says Kemadrin/procyclidine would be removed and replaced with Sinemet, escalated to 12.5/50 mg QDS.
16. Later years again showed medication-state effects in the feet/lower limbs, including OFF/dystonic reasoning and eventual severe morning foot pain improving once Parkinson medication reached the system.

### Patient voice

Liam now clarifies that the procyclidine response felt like a profound release of longstanding bodily tension, with a return of powerful, near-normal walking and a global sense of physical freedom.

He recalls that when the drug was withdrawn, the **first pain to return** was the focal pain on the outside of the left foot, the same pain he dates back to 2004. He says that focal pain then persisted until March 2025.

The present recollection should be read beside, not substituted for, the October 2017 emails. The emails directly corroborate the broader treatment-response state: dramatic improvement on Kemadrin/procyclidine, planned withdrawal to test effect, deterioration during withdrawal, foot restlessness during withdrawal, and later re-escalation because the lower dose did not cover all symptoms.

### Source-status boundary

- The October 2017 emails are contemporaneous patient-origin evidence.
- The statement that the outside-left-foot pain was the *first* symptom to return is present patient recollection unless independently corroborated by a contemporaneous note/email/witness.
- The proposition does **not** assert that procyclidine proved the feet were dystonia.
- It does **not** assert that procyclidine and levodopa are pharmacologically interchangeable.
- It asserts the narrower point that Neurology's own treatment materially altered the feet and therefore generated a clinical question about what that treatment-foot interaction meant.

### Expert question

> **What clinical significance should a treating neurologist attach when a planned withdrawal of antiparkinsonian medication is followed by deterioration that includes the feet, particularly where the foot/neurological relationship had previously been left unresolved? What assessment, documentation or reconsideration would good neurological practice ordinarily require?**

## Combined hinge between Propositions 1 and 2

The two propositions interact:

> **If the treatment rule was to avoid dopamine replacement `for as long as possible`, then evidence that Neurology's own medication materially changed the feet was potentially relevant to deciding what ongoing disability counted toward the endpoint of that rule. The surviving record does not presently show the feet being incorporated into a defined reopening threshold.**
