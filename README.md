## Ten-minute read: what this case is really about

This repository concerns a young-onset movement-disorder case in which the **evidence changed repeatedly, but the governing clinical frame appears not to have changed with it**.

The strongest current formulation is:

> **The case was not simply misdiagnosed. A management frame crystallised early, became more durable than the evidence beneath it, and repeatedly preserved the separation of the patient's feet from the neurological disease model even when treatment-response evidence kept reconnecting them.**

That distinction matters. The case does not require hindsight perfection. It does not require proving that every historic foot symptom was dystonia, that levodopa had to be started in July 2017, or that any clinician acted in bad faith. It asks a narrower and more forensic question:

> **What evidence was capable of reopening the frame once it had been set?**

The present record suggests that a surprising amount of evidence accumulated without restoring genuine interpretive freedom.

---

## 1. The patient entered Neurology through gait, feet and tension

The story did not begin with a tremor. It began with a long-standing focal pain on the **outside of the left foot**, later joined by worsening gait, bilateral foot problems, muscle tension, impaired initiation of movement and loss of function.

Primary care did not send a generic Parkinson's referral. The GP had already started connecting the **gait, feet and `muscle tension`** neurologically.

By July 2017, severe Parkinsonism was clinically obvious: marked rigidity, gross bradykinesia, abnormal gait, severe functional impairment and a lower-limb presentation that was at least partly dystonic-looking. The hospital also documented real structural foot pathology—corns, calluses, mallet/hammertoe-type deformity and pressure areas.

The key point is not that the clinicians had to choose one explanation and reject the other.

The record itself said the relationship was:

> **`Not clear.`**

That should have preserved uncertainty.

Instead, the practical architecture quickly became divided: Neurology managed the Parkinsonism; Podiatry managed the feet.

---

## 2. On or around 4 July 2017, the governing treatment frame appears to crystallise

During the first admission, the case went to a Neurology Complex Case Meeting, likely on Tuesday 4 July 2017.

The contemporaneous treatment note links Prof Timothy Counihan and Neurology colleagues directly to future caution with dopamine agonists and levodopa. The stated rationale included the extraordinarily young estimated onset—about age 24/26.

The crucial wording is not merely `caution`.

It is:

> **`We were likely try and avoid these for as long as possible.`**

Monaghan later wrote to Healy:

> **`Myself and Tim are keen to hold back on Levodopa insofar as maybe possible unless you'd advise otherwise?`**

The policy was therefore open-ended. What the record does not presently show is the thing that makes an open-ended rule clinically governable: **what would end it?**

No review date is visible. No disability threshold. No failed-treatment trigger. No medication-response trigger. No specialist-opinion trigger. No rule saying what would make further waiting no longer acceptable.

The patient recalls a bedside conversation after the meeting in which Monaghan said, colloquially:

> **`My boss told me not to give you the good stuff, and in time I'm afraid we might come to regret it.`**

He also recalls being told, in substance, that Monaghan did not want him returning years later saying his life could have been better.

That recollection remains patient evidence, not proof of Counihan's exact words. But it matters because the documentary record independently places Counihan and the Complex Case Meeting inside the treatment-caution architecture and independently records Monaghan's concern about future regret.

The central issue is therefore not whether caution was legitimate. It plainly had a rationale.

The issue is whether **a provisional caution became a durable governing rule without a documented reopening mechanism**.

---

## 3. Within months, Neurology's own treatment generated evidence against the separation

The first important contradiction arrived almost immediately.

Procyclidine/Kemadrin was prescribed by Neurology. In contemporaneous October 2017 emails, Liam described a striking response:

- his walk came back together;
- his legs became `loose and limber`;
- writing and ordinary dexterity improved;
- he could stand straight and move normally again;
- he described the experience as `living in a miracle`.

A planned medication break then produced the opposite pattern: tension rebuilt, the feet became restless and the walk began to fall apart.

Liam now recalls something even more anatomically specific: the first familiar focal pain to return was the pain on the **outside of the left foot**, the same site he had known since his mid-20s.

The present recollection is important because it materially agrees with contemporaneous self-report from 2017. The exact `first symptom` sequence still belongs in patient-source evidence unless independently corroborated, but the broader treatment-response state is contemporaneously documented.

This gives the case an unusually clean early experiment:

> **GP referral: gait + feet + tension**
> **Neurological treatment: procyclidine**
> **Observed response: gait, tension and feet improve together**
> **Withdrawal: the system deteriorates again**

Whatever the precise mechanism, Neurology had shown that the referred symptom complex could move together under neurological medication.

That does not prove every corn or callus was neurological. In fact, one important possibility is mixed causation: abnormal lower-limb tone or posture could alter loading, create or aggravate focal mechanical pain, and therefore make a structurally real corn hurt less when neurological tone improves.

The key question is simpler:

> **What did Neurology learn from the response?**

The surviving record does not presently show that this medication-foot interaction caused the Podiatry/Neurology partition—or the `for as long as possible` policy—to be formally reconsidered.

## 4. Healy strengthened PARK2, but the original management permission did not visibly move

In September 2017, Prof Healy reviewed Liam and wrote:

> **`I think probably has Park II.`**

He recommended PARK2 testing and described an approach that is important to distinguish from indefinite withholding:

> patients with PARK II may manage well for long periods with **relatively low doses of dopamine replacement therapy**, while over-use may cause dyskinesia.

That is a treatment strategy, not a tablet-size description.

`Relatively low dose` only has clinical meaning if it is tied to things like total daily exposure, titration speed, response, dyskinesia, duration and monitoring. Otherwise it risks becoming as elastic as `for as long as possible`.

By April 2018, Galway still recorded PARK2/PRKN as the suspected cause. The record acknowledged that Liam could be **`very much better`** on levodopa or another dopamine agonist, while maintaining that `we do not want to do that - at least for quite some time`.

The same note treated confirmation of suspected PARK2 as not particularly management-changing and said there was `no great loss` in not knowing immediately.

That is one of the clearest signs of path dependence in the case:

> **Would knowing the cause change the management plan we have already decided to preserve?**

rather than:

> **Does the probable cause require us to reconsider the management plan?**

The diagnostic belief moved. The management permission did not visibly move with it.

---

## 5. In 2021, levodopa finally entered—but not through a clean reassessment of the 2017 rule

By September/October 2021, Liam felt undertreated.

Sinemet 12.5/50 mg was introduced at BD dosing and moved to QDS within roughly two weeks. At the same time, raised ocular pressure meant Kemadrin/procyclidine had to be removed.

The October 2021 record is explicit that Sinemet was being used to **replace Kemadrin** and was escalated in the hope that it would compensate for the loss of the earlier drug.

That matters because Sinemet was not entering a blank system. It was inheriting therapeutic work previously done by procyclidine.

Within another two months, the regimen had moved to Stalevo 75/18.75/200 QDS. The prescription rationale included:

> **`re-emerging dystonia and altered gait`**

and described a `small increase in levodopa`.

The legal/clinical question is not whether those doses were definitely excessive. It is whether the actual longitudinal strategy still resembled the cautious, response-led, `relatively low-dose over long periods` PARK2 model Healy had described—and, if so, where that strategy was documented.

The nominal strength of the first tablet is not the answer.

## 6. By March 2023, the separated-foot model had become an urgent clinical problem

This is one of the strongest later hinges in the case.

The old focal outside-left-foot pain had by then expanded into **severe bilateral burning pain in both feet and lower legs**.

On 26 March 2023, Liam's GP sent an **urgent Neurology referral** directly to Monaghan. The referral described severe bilateral burning pain, extreme distress and a `death wish`, and asked for urgent review **`before we get into trouble here`**.

Two days later, Neurology's internal correspondence recorded:

- pregabalin/Lyrica at **600 mg/day**;
- debilitating pain;
- no quality of life;
- worsening over the previous months.

The significance is not simply that Lyrica `did not work`.

By 2023 the pain was being actively treated under the **neuropathic-pain model at very high intensity**, while Liam was also on regular immediate-release levodopa. Severe pain persisted under both pathways.

That should have created a mechanism question:

> **What exactly is generating this pain, and how does it behave across medication state?**

Then the record supplies the next answer.

In September 2023, Liam reported severe foot pain, reduced mobility and shuffling, worse in the morning and worse when Sinemet had not been taken.

A movement-disorders clinician wrote:

> **`It sounds like his Parkinson's is being undertreated and the leg pain and shuffling is off-related.`**

Monaghan agreed that a Stalevo trial was reasonable.

That is a major longitudinal convergence:

- **2017:** Neurological medication changes gait, tension and feet together.
- **March 2023:** High-dose neuropathic treatment is failing while pain becomes severe and bilateral.
- **September 2023:** The pain/mobility syndrome is explicitly identified as OFF-related.

The later evidence did not create a new relationship. It reproduced an old one that had already been visible in 2017.

---

## 7. By 2025, the evidential asymmetry is explicit in Monaghan's own words

The March 2025 clinic note records the feet as the dominant problem. Liam describes the first steps of the morning as `torture` and says he will not live with the pain.

Yet the neuropathy hypothesis remains conditional:

> **`If there is a degree of peripheral neuropathy here, the alcohol being gone will hopefully slow the deterioration of that.`**

That wording matters: **if** and **hopefully**.

In the same clinical period, Monaghan documents something much less conditional:

> once Parkinson medication reaches the system, the feet improve;

> Liam knows Sinemet makes the feet feel better from a pain perspective;

> Monaghan has little doubt that more Sinemet would probably give further pain relief.

So by 2025 the evidential positions are asymmetrical:

- neuropathy: possible;
- alcohol contribution: possible / hoped-for benefit;
- dopaminergic foot-pain relief: observed;
- further dopaminergic benefit: expected by the treating neurologist.

Then controlled-release Sinemet produces major relief reported by the patient.

In the later rebuttal, written in response to criticism of anchoring, the language shifts from `if there is a degree of peripheral neuropathy` to a more settled formulation of `peripheral neuropathic pain symptomatologies`, while still acknowledging strong dopaminergic modification.

The presently identified record does not show the intervening objective event by which the conditional hypothesis became more certain.

That is not proof of dishonesty. It is a clean documentary question about **where the increase in certainty came from**.

## 8. The patient voice is not decorative; it explains how the frame governed behaviour

Liam's present account has an important feature: in several places it agrees with contemporaneous evidence written years before complaint or litigation.

That gives the patient voice evidential weight as **retrospective recollection with contemporaneous corroboration**.

The most useful patient-side skeleton is anatomical and longitudinal.

Liam says the outside of the left foot is where the condition first `grabbed` him in his mid-20s. He can still identify the exact site. He understood later compensatory movement, imbalance and contortion as attempts by the rest of his body to offset what that focal problem was doing.

For him, that pain became the body's earliest and most reliable warning signal.

So when procyclidine produced profound relaxation and then withdrawal brought the old foot pain back, he experienced that as the condition `talking` through the feet.

This matters later because the feet were not an incidental symptom to him. They were his **primary sensor for the state of the whole motor system**.

The trust chronology is equally important.

Liam says the dramatic early treatment response convinced him that he was in good hands. He trusted the neurologist, liked the nursing team and remained within that clinical model until the morning of **14 March 2025**.

That means years without formal protest should not automatically be read as years of informed agreement with the explanatory model. His operating position was trust.

By 2023–2025, he says repeated discussion of the feet through neuropathy and alcohol had narrowed his expectation that reporting the feet to Neurology would produce useful reconsideration. He continued to tell his GP.

The March 2023 urgent GP referral therefore becomes more than a clinical document. It is the route through which the severity of the feet is forced back into Neurology after the patient has begun to lose confidence that raising them there will change anything.

The later 2025 writings show a man under severe strain, but also repeatedly trying to remain structured: seeking GP review, psychiatric support, medication guidance, documenting timing and response, and trying to create a coherent account of what his body is doing.

The intensity of the later record should therefore not be mistaken for the patient's starting condition.

The earlier record shows endurance, adaptation, work, minimisation and trust.

The later intensity is part of the damage chronology.

## 9. The simplest current case theory

The case can now be stated in one sequence:

1. **A frame crystallised early.** On or around 4 July 2017, conventional dopaminergic treatment was to be avoided `for as long as possible`, while the foot/lower-limb relationship remained unresolved.
2. **Neurology's own treatment challenged the separation almost immediately.** Procyclidine changed gait, tension and the feet together.
3. **Specialist evidence moved toward PARK2.** Healy said probable PARK2 and described cautious low-dose dopamine replacement over long periods.
4. **The original management architecture persisted.** By 2018 the suspected cause had changed, but current management was treated as essentially unaffected.
5. **Levodopa finally entered in 2021 as a replacement for Kemadrin and escalated quickly.** The record cited re-emerging dystonia and altered gait.
6. **By 2023 the separated-foot model was failing clinically.** Severe bilateral pain persisted despite pregabalin 600 mg/day and regular levodopa; the GP escalated urgently.
7. **The record then explicitly identified an OFF-related mechanism.** Pain and shuffling worsened when Sinemet was absent, and a movement-disorders clinician called the syndrome undertreated Parkinsonism / OFF-related.
8. **By 2025 neuropathy remained conditional while dopaminergic relief was observed.** The competing explanation had less patient-specific evidential support than the medication-state relationship actually being seen.
9. **The patient then obtained major relief through altered dopaminergic coverage.** The later response does not retrospectively prove what had to be done in 2017, but it does make the absence of earlier documented reassessment highly material.
10. **In 2026 genetics confirmed the earlier PRKN branch.** St Vincent's concluded that Liam's Parkinsonism and dystonia were due to compound heterozygous Parkin mutations.

The deepest question is therefore not:

> **Why did nobody notice the feet?**

The record shows that the feet were noticed repeatedly.

It is:

> **Why did repeated evidence that neurological treatment altered the feet fail to give the feet corresponding authority inside the neurological disease model?**

Or even more simply:

> **The evidence remained dynamic; the frame became static.**

---

## 10. What this case does *not* need to prove

The case is stronger if it stays disciplined.

It does **not** need to prove:

- every historic foot symptom was dystonia;
- every corn/callus was neurological;
- Counihan ordered prolonged non-use of levodopa;
- Healy ordered immediate levodopa;
- a levodopa challenge was mandatory in July 2017;
- pregabalin failure disproves neuropathy;
- procyclidine and levodopa are mechanistically interchangeable;
- the 2025 response proves the 2017 standard of care;
- deliberate concealment, bad faith or dishonesty.

What the case can properly ask is whether **uncertainty was allowed to remain genuinely corrigible**.

That is the core counsel-facing issue.

## Current controlling propositions

1. **The July 2017 dopaminergic-deferral rule became controlling without a documented reopening mechanism.**
2. **Neurology's own treatment functionally demonstrated that the GP-referred gait–feet–tension complex could move together under neurological medication.**
3. **The feet were not merely omitted; their operational separation was repeatedly preserved despite stronger treatment-response evidence than the competing neuropathy/alcohol hypothesis.**
4. **The next proposition under development concerns the 2021–2025 treatment transition: whether worsening bilateral foot pain, rapid levodopa escalation, failed high-dose neuropathic treatment and later OFF-related evidence should have forced a structured reassessment of pain mechanism and medication-state dependence.**
