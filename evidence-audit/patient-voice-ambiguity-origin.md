# Patient Voice, Ambiguity, and the Origin of the `Separate Feet` Question

**Function:** reconstruct the ambiguity around the feet/lower limbs by reading the institutional record alongside Liam's own recorded explanations.

## Core finding

The record does not support a simple proposition that Liam consistently said `the feet were separate from the neurological disorder`.

What it records is more nuanced.

Liam repeatedly distinguishes **different experiences within the lower-limb/foot presentation**: local foot pain, corns/calluses, burning, tension, clawing, gait architecture, tightness, and later medication-state effects. The institutional record then sometimes expresses those distinctions through the broader language of a `separate problem`.

The live reconstruction question is therefore:

> **Was a valid patient distinction between different symptoms transformed into a broader clinical distinction between different disease processes?**

That is a materially different proposition.

---

## 1. The GP referral: the ambiguity begins inside one presenting problem

The 29 June 2017 GP referral records Liam attending with bilateral foot issues and attributing his abnormal gait at that time to calluses and corns. But the same referral records increasing difficulty initiating movement, marked stiffness and tone in all four limbs, brisk reflexes, toe clawing, inability to relax and deteriorating dexterity/work function.

The GP's question was not `podiatry or Parkinson's`.

It was an open integrated question:

`?parkinsonian or other underlying diagnosis`

Thus the record enters hospital with both local foot explanations and neurological signs already occupying the same presenting problem.

---

## 2. Liam's 2017 explanation is a distinction between experiences

The discharge history records Liam looking back over the sequence and describing:

- longstanding bodily/leg `tension`;
- later corns, calluses and fallen arches in both feet;
- treatment for the feet from about 2009/2010;
- reduction of foot pain after leaving bar work and spending less time on his feet;
- persistence of the `leg tension` despite that reduction in foot pain.

The record then says that in 2012 he began to realise that `the foot pain and the leg tension were not the same problem` because the foot pain settled/reduced while the tension remained.

That is important.

It is a phenomenological and temporal distinction. Liam is explaining why he did not experience **foot pain** and **leg tension** as identical phenomena.

It does not, by itself, establish that:

- all phenomena occurring in the feet were non-neurological;
- the painful feet had no neurological component;
- the dystonic-looking lower-limb posture was unrelated to the feet;
- the structural abnormalities and the neurological disorder could not coexist or interact;
- the foot/lower-limb syndrome had been etiologically divided into two independent diseases.

---

## 3. The clinical record broadens the distinction

Later in the same discharge architecture, the clinician writes that:

`The leg symptoms including corns and callouses and burning may have been completely separate or perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy ... Not clear`

Immediately afterwards the record says Liam had `in his own mind - markedly divorced the feelings of tension in the legs ... from the corns and callouses issues and pains in the feet`.

This passage contains an important ambiguity of attribution and scope.

Liam's recorded reasoning supports the narrower proposition:

> **the tension and the local foot pain did not behave identically.**

The clinician's preceding formulation raises the broader proposition:

> **the leg/foot symptoms may have been completely separate from the neurological syndrome.**

Those are not the same proposition.

The record itself does not close the gap between them. It ends the etiological question with `Not clear`.

---

## 4. A plausible origin for the `separate feet` architecture

The institutional question may therefore have arisen through a sequence like this:

1. Liam reports real structural foot problems and pain.
2. Liam explains that reducing time on his feet reduced the pain.
3. He separately reports that neurological-feeling `tension` persisted.
4. He therefore tells clinicians that the pain and tension are not the same experience/problem.
5. The record considers whether the foot/leg phenomena may therefore be `completely separate` or peripheral.
6. Because the neurological relationship is unresolved, the final record says `Not clear`.
7. Podiatry nevertheless becomes the practical foot pathway.

The important possible transformation is:

**different symptoms → different problem → separate pathology → separate pathway.**

Each step is broader than the one before it.

The repository should test where, if anywhere, that broadening was clinically established rather than merely inferred.

---

## 5. The physical examination complicates any simple separation

The same 2017 record describes:

- a strikingly abnormal gait;
- a footdrop-like appearance `somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture - NOT FOOT DROP`;
- the left lower limb kicking out like foot drop;
- gross bradykinesia and rigidity;
- pes planus and hammertoes;
- minimal tremor;
- young-onset Parkinsonism;
- possible recessive/genetic Parkinson's disease.

Thus the record contains both structural/local foot pathology and neurological lower-limb phenomenology at the same time.

A patient saying `the corn pain is not the same thing as the tension` is entirely compatible with that mixed picture.

It does not logically resolve whether part of what he experienced in or through the feet was neurological.

---

## 6. The 2018 record shows why Liam's distinctions matter

By April 2018 Liam's main physical concerns include foot cramps and hand cramps. He describes the relief from tension as `fantastic` and the ability to walk without tension as a major improvement.

The record also preserves the Procyclidine withdrawal episode: after Procyclidine was stopped, the first rapidly changing feature Liam noticed was the return/change at the outside-left-foot/corn site; symptoms worsened and he became more `shifty` and `unrelaxed`.

This does not prove that the corn itself was neurological.

It demonstrates why the patient's own distinction should have been investigated rather than flattened. A local anatomical site associated with a structural corn could also participate in a medication-sensitive neurological experience.

The correct model may therefore have been **mixed**, not binary.

---

## 7. By 2023 Liam is still trying to describe more than one thing

The 2023 record is especially revealing because Liam repeatedly tries to distinguish:

- residual local/coronal foot pain;
- clawing;
- the `pain of the attention it takes to walk`;
- inability to perform the fourth evening walk;
- the `architecture of the walking`;
- OFF-like lack of movement;
- the effect of Stalevo, which he says he takes for both `Pep in the Step` and `for pain`.

The clinician explicitly considers whether the cramping could represent Parkinsonian dystonia, more likely OFF dystonia, and whether the evening walking failure reflects insufficient levodopa.

At the same time, Liam is recorded as believing that a `separate problem` involving the feet constitutes a major part of what is happening. The clinician describes a `misalignment`: his own firm Parkinson's-centred belief versus Liam's `reasonable and justifiable concerns` that much relates to the feet as a separate problem.

That phrase should not be read without the rest of Liam's account.

His recorded model is not simply `feet are unrelated to Parkinson's`.

He is simultaneously reporting local pain, clawing, movement-architecture difficulty, OFF-like timing, and levodopa use for pain and movement.

The dispute is therefore partly about **how to partition a mixed phenotype**, not about choosing between two mutually exclusive stories.

---

## 8. By 2025 Liam supplies a direct causal hypothesis

The 2025 note records severe morning foot pain: the first steps are `torture`, the feet are too sore to tie runners, and once Parkinson's medication reaches his system he experiences relief in the feet.

Most importantly for patient voice, Liam himself proposes that an element of the morning problem may be **how bad the Parkinson's is expected to be in the morning because he has had no medication overnight**.

That is a patient-generated causal hypothesis.

The clinician then develops precisely that possibility operationally by proposing overnight/controlled-release Sinemet and early-morning dosing while also preserving competing explanations and dyskinesia risk.

This is the record becoming interested in Liam's explanatory model in a way that is directly testable through treatment.

---

## 9. The larger interpretive correction

The repository should stop using `Liam thought the feet were separate` as though it were a stable, unitary proposition across the eight years.

The source record supports a more accurate formulation:

> **Liam consistently tried to distinguish different components of his experience. He recognised that structural/local foot pain and the bodily/leg tension did not behave identically. Later he continued to distinguish pain, clawing, gait architecture, medication state and local foot symptoms while questioning how much belonged to the neurological disorder. The institutional record sometimes translated those distinctions into the broader category of a `separate foot problem`.**

That translation is itself part of the case to be investigated.

---

## 10. Questions generated by the patient's voice

1. When Liam said the `foot pain and leg tension were not the same problem`, what exactly did he mean by `problem`?
2. Did clinicians distinguish `not the same symptom` from `not the same cause`?
3. When the later note says Liam had `markedly divorced` tension from foot pain, is that a faithful account of his intended causal position or a clinician's summary of a narrower phenomenological distinction?
4. Which aspects of the feet did Liam regard as structural/local and which did he regard as part of the movement/tension problem?
5. Did he ever say that **all** foot phenomena were unrelated to Parkinsonism?
6. When did clawing, dystonic posture, cramping, gait architecture and medication-state effects become explicitly connected to his understanding of the feet?
7. Did the Podiatry referral receive Liam's own distinction, or only the broader `foot problem` category?
8. Did later clinicians inherit `separate feet` as a historical fact when the 2017 source had only recorded an unresolved hypothesis?
9. When Liam protested at the bedside, was he specifically objecting to this broadening — i.e. to clinicians converting his distinction between pain and tension into a conclusion that the feet belonged outside the neurological problem?
10. Can the four student witnesses help recover exactly what Liam was trying to explain at that moment?

---

## Governing formulation

> **The ambiguity may have originated not because Liam supplied a confused history, but because he supplied a discriminating one: local foot pain changed when mechanical exposure changed, while tension and movement abnormality persisted. The forensic question is whether that accurate distinction between experiences was subsequently enlarged into an unsupported distinction between causes.**

This question should now guide reconstruction of the bedside protest, Podiatry referral, later `misalignment`, and all uses of `separate` language in the counsel presentation.
