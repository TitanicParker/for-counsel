The Case Issues for Counsel Source Records

Counsel orientation

The case began with the feet.

The central question is not whether Parkinsonism was real. It was. The question is what happened to an unresolved painful foot and lower-limb problem once Parkinson’s disease became the operative framework for care.

1. The case entered hospital as an open problem

On 29 June 2017 a GP urgently referred a 39-year-old man whose gait, movement and dexterity were deteriorating badly enough to threaten his employment. The referral described an unusual festinant and stiff gait, bilateral foot problems, corns and calluses, difficulty initiating movement, marked stiffness and increased tone, brisk reflexes and declining function. It did not present a settled diagnosis. The question posed was whether the deterioration was Parkinsonian or reflected some other underlying disorder.

That matters because the feet were not incidental history added years later. They were within the accepted presenting problem from the beginning.

Evidence: F0001–F0011 · GP referral

2. Neurology found severe Parkinsonism — and also preserved important uncertainty

The neurological findings were striking. The record describes markedly increased symmetrical tone, gross bradykinesia, rigidity and a profoundly abnormal gait. A later DaTSCAN was abnormal and supported a presynaptic dopaminergic disorder. Any serious account of this case has to begin by accepting those findings rather than trying to explain them away.

But the same assessment also records an unusual lower-limb picture. The gait had a footdrop-like appearance described as somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture — expressly “NOT FOOT DROP”. The patient also had pes planus, hammertoes, corns, calluses and burning pain. The record considered whether the leg and foot symptoms might be separate, perhaps peripheral, and then gave the most important contemporary answer available: “Not clear.”

The uncertainty was therefore not whether severe Parkinsonism existed. It concerned what else was present, what caused it, and how the painful foot and lower-limb presentation related to the neurological syndrome.

Evidence: F0012–F0025 · 2017 discharge / extended consult record · 2017 Neurology / DaTSCAN

3. A practical framework nevertheless had to be chosen

Parkinson’s disease became the operative diagnosis. Treatment strategy, follow-up, work advice and LTI administration were organised within that framework. Conventional dopaminergic treatment was deliberately deferred because of the patient’s age and the anticipated risks of dyskinesia, impulse-control disorder and dopamine dysregulation. Procyclidine was used symptomatically. Podiatry was separately requested for the feet.

None of those steps is self-evidently improper. A working diagnosis has to guide care, treatment choices can reasonably be cautious, and structural foot pathology may properly require podiatry even in a neurological patient.

The documentary difficulty is narrower. The same record that made those pathways operative still described the relationship between the feet and the neurological syndrome as unresolved.

Evidence: F0028–F0036 · 2017 discharge / extended consult record

4. The discharge summary is a cumulative document

The completed discharge artefact should not automatically be treated as a single snapshot of everything known and decided at one moment. The operative discharge and LTI state preceded the formal 13 July DaTSCAN result. The scan result was later incorporated into the same clinical artefact by addendum.

That is not evidence of improper alteration. Cumulative clinical records are normal. But it matters for forensic reading: a finished document can look more coherent than the chronological decision sequence that produced it.

This is the first place where the working concept of administrative crystallization becomes useful. It describes a hypothesis to test, not a concluded breach: a clinically plausible Parkinsonian diagnosis had become sufficiently definite to govern practical decisions while substantial questions about aetiology and the foot/lower-limb phenotype remained within the same record.

Evidence: F0034, F0037–F0040 · native EHR version history remains outstanding

5. PRKN was already inside the diagnostic architecture

The later genetic diagnosis did not introduce Parkin disease into a history in which nobody had thought of it. The early record considered autosomal-recessive Parkinsonism and specifically PRKN/PARK2. By April 2018 the working record went further, recording that a specific PARK2/PRKN test or gene panel could be used and describing PRKN as what “we/Prof Healy suspect is the cause”.

That does not mean PRKN had been diagnosed. Nor does the present repository independently establish Prof Healy’s exact wording without his native response. It does establish something important about the treating record itself: a genetic Parkin explanation was a live possibility well before the 2026 result.

Evidence: F0021, F0052 and later PRKN/Healy fact-register entries · 2018 Neurology record · 2017 Healy correspondence

6. The first treatment-response evidence arrived early

In September or October 2017 Procyclidine was stopped for about two weeks because of visual blurring. The April 2018 Neurology history records that the first thing noticed changing rapidly during that withdrawal involved the outside-left-foot/corn site; symptoms worsened somewhat and the patient felt more “shifty” and “unrelaxed”.

A separate contemporaneous email from 24 October 2017 independently records the medication stop and restart, incomplete symptom control at the reduced dose, planned dose escalation and an active podiatry appointment. In the same email the patient described himself as physically very much improved and compared normal movement and function to “living in a miracle.”

Those records do not prove that a corn was neurological, or that every later foot symptom was dystonia. They establish the more modest point that very early in the course, part of the symptom complex involving the foot changed with neurological medication.

The resulting question is prospective rather than retrospective: what significance, if any, should that have had for a relationship previously recorded as “Not clear”?

Evidence: F0041–F0048 · 2018 Neurology record · 24 October 2017 email

7. The following years do not support a simple one-cause theory

The longitudinal record is mixed. Later clinicians again observed parkinsonian lower-limb phenomena. Foot pain remained a major problem. OFF dystonia was considered. Evening walking difficulty was linked to inadequate levodopa at times, and Stalevo was used for both “pep in the step” and pain. By 2023 the record expressly acknowledges a “misalignment” between the clinician’s Parkinson’s-centred explanation and the patient’s concern that much of the problem remained centred in the feet.

But other evidence cuts the other way. Structural foot abnormalities existed. Toe supports helped. Lyrica helped pain. A later medication-withdrawal episode did not produce the neat Parkinsonian change that a unitary dopamine-tracked theory would predict. These facts matter because the strongest case is not that every painful foot symptom since 2004 was dystonia. It is that the symptom complex remained mixed and repeatedly returned to neurological treatment states.

Evidence: F0050, F0053–F0064 and later 2024/adverse fact-register entries · 2023 Neurology record · 2024 Neurology record

8. March 2025 created a new evidential event

On 10 March 2025 the central problem in clinic was again the feet. The record describes severe morning pain: difficulty tying runners, “murder in the feet”, and the first roughly 150 footsteps as torture. It also records something clinically important: once Parkinson’s medication reached the system, the feet felt better from a pain perspective.

The neurologist did not interpret that as proof that the pain itself was directly Parkinsonian. He expressly said the described pain was not, in his view, direct Parkinson’s phenomenology, while recognising that dystonia can be painful and predicting that more Sinemet would probably provide further relief. The difficulty was dyskinesia.

The practical response was a trial of night-time Sinemet CR aimed at the severe morning experience. In his later complaint response the neurologist expressly said his 10 March prescription was also intended to help the morning experience including pain. The later response was described as “profound relief currently.”

Evidence: F0065–F0075 plus later March-2025 response facts · 2025 Neurology record

9. The treatment response then changed the patient’s understanding

Nine days later, on 19 March 2025, the patient made a formal complaint. He described the recent dopamine-responsive experience as a turning point and asked for an independent review of the preceding years. That complaint is important even before anyone decides whether its medical allegations are right. It is contemporaneous evidence of when and why the patient began to reinterpret the earlier history.

It is also important adverse evidence for limitation analysis. The complaint shows that by 19 March 2025 the patient was expressly alleging historical diagnostic anchoring and treatment-related harm. Later discoveries may add proof or connections, but they cannot simply erase what he already understood by that date.

Evidence: F0076–F0083 · 19 March 2025 complaint

10. The later genetic result explains Parkinsonism and dystonia together

In February 2026 a movement-disorders specialist reported two pathogenic Parkin variants and concluded that the patient’s Parkinsonism and dystonia were due to compound heterozygous Parkin mutations.

The result supplies later etiological coherence to two features that had already existed in the earlier record: Parkinsonism and dystonic phenomenology. It does not prove that clinicians in 2017 possessed the 2026 answer. It does not prove that every historic foot symptom was dystonia. And it does not, by itself, decide negligence.

Its significance is more disciplined: the later confirmed cause belongs to a diagnostic family the earlier record had already contemplated.

Evidence: F0093–F0097 · 2026 PRKN genetic result

11. The institutional review reached a no-deficit conclusion

The hospital later subjected the complaint to a Preliminary Assessment Report and SIMT process. The outcome records that the multidisciplinary SIMT considered the care appropriate and concluded there was “no deficit in the care provided.” That decision is part of the evidence and must be presented fairly.

What cannot yet be reconstructed from the material presently available is the proposition-level evidential architecture beneath that conclusion. The complete PAR, source schedule and attachments, together with the SIMT agenda, minutes and circulation pack, have not been located in the current corpus.

The safe formulation is therefore neither that SIMT failed to revalidate the disputed foot/podiatry hinge nor that it independently did so. It is that independent revalidation of that hinge has not yet been demonstrated from the materials presently available.

Evidence: F0084–F0092, F0104–F0105 · SIMT outcome

12. The question now presented

This is therefore not best understood as a claim that the Parkinsonian diagnosis was implausible. It plainly was plausible and strongly supported. Nor is it a claim that podiatry was necessarily wrong, that levodopa necessarily had to begin in 2017, or that the later genetic result should be projected backwards.

The narrower question is what happened when a plausible Parkinsonian diagnosis became the operative framework for care while important questions within the same presentation — particularly aetiology and the relationship of the painful foot/lower-limb syndrome to the neurological disorder — remained unresolved.

Question for expert and legal review. When, if at all, should the later treatment-response, dystonia and genetic evidence have caused that unresolved classification to be reconsidered; and what consequences, if any, followed from the way the original framework continued to govern care, information and later review?

This page is an explanatory orientation. It deliberately separates documentary propositions from medical standard, breach, causation, limitation and legal conclusions, which require independent expert and counsel analysis.
