Pending primary evidence

The Podiatry question has narrowed.

The awaited records do not need to decide whether “the feet were Parkinson's.” They need to show what Podiatry was actually asked to treat inside a case already being routed to it “as Parkinson's,” what information travelled, what Podiatry found and did, and whether anything returned to Neurology.

What was already true before the Podiatry pathway matured

Before admission, the GP record moved from bilateral foot pain, abnormal gait and real structural pathology to consideration of Neurology for the patient's “gait, feet and 'muscle tension' issues”. Occupational Health then echoed the gait concern and Neurology referral proceeded.

During admission, Neurology documented severe young-onset Parkinsonism, a strikingly abnormal gait, a footdrop-like lower-limb appearance somewhat suggestive of dystonic posture, genuine structural foot abnormalities and an explicit PRKN/PARK2 differential. The relationship between the foot/leg phenomena and the neurological syndrome was still recorded as “Not clear.”

A new hard fact: 6 July 2017

GP record: “Phoned podiatry to discuss case - will mark as priority case and as Parkinson's - should be called mid September.”

This changes the referral question. The GP-side record does not describe Podiatry as handling an unrelated routine foot problem. It records the case being prioritised “as Parkinson's.”

The referral-content question

First question: what did the written referral actually say?

The original referral should establish whether Podiatry was told about Parkinsonism, the dystonic-looking gait, the unresolved neurological relationship, burning/pain, corns/calluses, pes planus/hammertoes, or concern that more than one process might be present.

The 6 July telephone note proves a Parkinson's framing existed in the GP-side routing. It does not prove what detail reached the Podiatry clinician. That distinction must be preserved.

Adjunctive care or explanatory substitution?

Podiatry may have been entirely appropriate for corns, calluses, pressure areas, pes planus, footwear mechanics and toe deformity. The key systems question is whether this was adjunctive local care while Neurology retained responsibility for the unresolved neurological relationship, or whether referral functionally became the answer to that relationship.

What exactly was Podiatry being asked to treat in a case already prioritised “as Parkinson's” while Neurology itself still recorded the relationship as “Not clear”?

The timing and protest question

Liam now fixes the reported bedside protest to Monday 10 July 2017, St Anthony's Ward, approximately 10:00–12:00. He says four medical students were together at the bedside on the first day of a new placement batch when he asked them to summon the neurologist.

He recalls challenging discharge with the analogy of receiving a “full NCT and [being sent] out on bald tires” and recalls the neurologist responding: “it's easy for you to get a podiatry appointment as me.”

This remains patient-source evidence until independently corroborated. The exact student-batch details now create a focused witness-identification route.

What did Podiatry think it was treating?

  • local callus/corn pressure?
  • pes planus or footwear mechanics?
  • toe deformity/clawing?
  • burning or neuropathic-type pain?
  • abnormal movement, dystonia or gait?
  • a mixed problem?

The later record shows that local and neurological phenomena could coexist. A local treatment response therefore cannot automatically explain the whole lower-limb/foot syndrome.

What treatment was delivered, and what changed?

The awaited notes should establish examination, debridement/paring if performed, padding/orthoses/supports, footwear advice, follow-up, symptom-specific response and reason for discharge from Podiatry.

The correct response question is component-specific: did a corn improve while toe curling, dystonic posture, lower-limb throwing, tension or medication-sensitive phenomena persisted?

Did information travel back?

Most important systems question: if Podiatry found anything inconsistent with a purely structural explanation, did that information return to Neurology?

Look for letters, discharge notes, telephone contacts, recommendations, requests for neurological review, or silence.

What would materially change the case?

Potentially supportive: a narrow structural referral despite the Parkinson's/dystonic context; Podiatry treating local lesions while abnormal movement persisted; failure of local treatment; Podiatry concern about abnormal movement/clawing; or feedback that did not trigger neurological reassessment.

Potentially qualifying/adverse: a detailed referral preserving neurological uncertainty; strong independent structural explanation for the disabling symptoms; sustained response to local treatment; or clear evidence that Neurology received and reconsidered Podiatry findings.
