Pending primary evidence

The Podiatry records should answer a narrow set of questions.

The case does not need Podiatry to prove that every foot symptom was neurological. It needs the records to show what problem Podiatry was actually asked to treat, what information travelled with the referral, what was found, what was done, and whether anything returned to Neurology.

What was already true before the referral

Before Podiatry became the practical pathway, the hospital had already documented severe young-onset Parkinsonism, a strikingly abnormal gait, a footdrop-like lower-limb appearance described as somewhat suggestive of dystonic posture, genuine structural foot abnormalities, and an explicit PRKN/PARK2 differential. The relationship between the leg/foot phenomena and the neurological syndrome was nevertheless recorded as “Not clear.”

That is the baseline against which the Podiatry material must be read.

The referral-content question

First question: what did the referral actually say?

The original referral should establish whether Podiatry was told about Parkinsonism, the dystonic-looking gait, the unresolved neurological relationship, burning/pain, corns/calluses, pes planus/hammertoes, or any concern that more than one process might be present.

If the referral reduced the problem to corns, calluses or routine structural care, that would be materially different from a referral that expressly preserved neurological uncertainty.

The timing and authorship question

The records should identify the referral date, author, destination and urgency. That matters because Liam reports a separate Monday-morning protest immediately before expected discharge, after asking the medical students at his bedside to summon the neurologist.

His recalled challenge was whether the neurologist intended to give him a “full NCT and send me out on bald tires”. He recalls the neurologist responding: “it's easy for you to get a podiatry appointment as me.”

This is patient-source evidence and remains to be tested independently. It is distinct from the earlier Tuesday-morning Complex Case Meeting communication.

What did Podiatry think it was treating?

The strongest Podiatry evidence will not be a generic attendance entry. It will be the clinician's own contemporaneous description of the problem presented to them:

  • local callus/corn pressure?
  • pes planus or footwear mechanics?
  • toe deformity/clawing?
  • burning or neuropathic-type pain?
  • abnormal movement, dystonia or gait?
  • a mixed problem?

The distinction matters because the later neurological record itself demonstrates that local and neurological phenomena could coexist.

What treatment was actually delivered?

The awaited notes should permit a clean treatment chronology: examination, debridement or paring if performed, padding/orthoses/supports, footwear advice, follow-up, response, and reason for discharge from Podiatry.

Liam's first-person account of treatment received should then be compared against the native notes rather than substituted for them.

Did the treatment work?

The correct question is symptom-specific. A local treatment may improve pressure pain or a corn while leaving toe curling, dystonic posture, lower-limb throwing, tension or medication-sensitive phenomena unchanged. Improvement in one component cannot automatically prove that the entire foot syndrome was local or peripheral.

Equally, failure of local treatment would not by itself prove a neurological cause. The point is to separate the components.

Did information travel back?

Most important systems question: if Podiatry found something inconsistent with the working partition, did that information return to Neurology?

The records should be checked for letters, discharge notes, telephone contacts, recommendations, requests for neurological review, or silence. This is the same reopening question that governs the wider case: what happened when the information state changed?

How the records should be classified when received

Each Podiatry item should be entered as a primary source before interpretation. Extract separately:

  • date and author;
  • referral wording;
  • history received;
  • findings;
  • diagnostic labels;
  • treatment performed;
  • response;
  • communications back;
  • closure/discharge reason.

Only after that should the material be mapped against the existing propositions.

What would materially change the case?

Potentially supportive: a referral that omits the neurological uncertainty; Podiatry treating a narrow structural problem while neurological features persisted; failure of local treatment; Podiatry concern about abnormal movement/clawing; or evidence sent back that did not trigger reassessment.

Potentially qualifying/adverse: a detailed referral preserving neurological uncertainty; strong independent structural explanation for the disabling symptoms; sustained response to local treatment; or clear evidence that Neurology received and reconsidered Podiatry findings.

Both categories belong in the repository.

The Podiatry records are not expected to decide whether “the feet were Parkinson's.” Their real value is to show what happened to a clinical question that Neurology itself had left “Not clear.”
