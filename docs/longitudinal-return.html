2020–2024

The unresolved problem keeps returning

The later record does not supply one clean mechanism. It supplies repeated reasons not to treat the feet as a settled historical side issue.

2020: the lower limb is again read neurologically

By 2020 the record again documents an outside-left-foot corn, marked sensitivity of the feet and excessive movement or urge in the feet. A left lower-limb “throw” that looked like foot drop was expressly characterised as parkinsonian because power for dorsiflexion, plantarflexion, inversion and eversion was normal.

This is not retrospective proof that the 2017 foot presentation was dystonia. It is a later independent neurological observation showing that unusual lower-limb phenomena continued to sit inside the movement-disorder picture.

2021–2022: levodopa enters, but the record remains mixed

Sinemet was introduced in September 2021 as the first levodopa drug and was escalated cautiously. The treatment record also preserves the reasons for restraint: young age, choreiform/dyskinetic effects and the broader risks associated with long-term dopaminergic exposure.

By 2022 the patient described the feet as taking twenty to twenty-five minutes to “warm up”, ongoing soreness around corns and calluses, and a marked reduction in cramping. Medication timing was adjusted around expected activity. These are clinically suggestive observations, but they do not eliminate mechanical foot pathology.

2023: the dispute becomes explicit

In 2023 severe bilateral foot pain became a dominant problem. Neurology considered possible OFF dystonia, and the record expressly describes a “misalignment” over whether the feet were a separate problem or part of the Parkinsonian picture. The same history records that the very first symptoms had been in the feet.

A separate urgent GP referral describes severe bilateral burning pain in the feet and lower legs and asks for urgent review. Parkinson nursing input also considered reduced mobility and shuffling in OFF terms, and Stalevo was introduced.

The same year also contains important adverse evidence

The 2023 record is not a simple dopamine-response story. In one nurse communication the patient had stopped Parkinson medication for around ten days and reported no difference “from a Parkinson’s perspective”, while severe foot and leg pain persisted.

That evidence matters. It weakens any proposition that all severe foot pain tracked dopamine state in a simple or continuous way. It supports a mixed, state-dependent or multi-mechanism model rather than an all-or-nothing theory.

2024: another non-dopaminergic treatment helps

By 2024 Lyrica was recorded as substantially reducing pain, while toe curling and evening medication timing remained relevant. Again, the most source-disciplined reading is mixed: mechanical/local, neuropathic, dystonic and Parkinson-related modulation may coexist.

The cumulative question

No single later event conclusively resolves the 2017 uncertainty. The significance lies in recurrence. The feet repeatedly return through neurological observations, medication-state changes, OFF considerations, pain treatment, movement abnormalities and patient challenge.

The expert question is therefore not whether one later note retrospectively proves the early classification wrong. It is whether the accumulation of later evidence crossed a threshold at which the original unresolved relationship required formal reconsideration.

Why this chapter matters. The strongest case is longitudinal and mixed. It becomes weaker, not stronger, if adverse evidence is omitted.
