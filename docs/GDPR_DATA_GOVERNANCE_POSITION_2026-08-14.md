# GDPR / Data-Governance Position — Actual Procedural Status vs Repository Potential

**Date:** 14 August 2026  
**Purpose:** controlling counsel-facing data-governance note  
**Scope:** HSE / Galway University Hospitals, University of Galway, PCRS and Medical Council GDPR strands  
**Status:** analytical control document. It is not a DPC decision, legal opinion, pleading, finding of GDPR infringement or finding of improper motive.

---

# 1. Controlling distinction

The GDPR material must be understood on two separate planes.

## A. Actual procedural status

What has actually been submitted, acknowledged, refused, referred or placed under regulatory assessment?

## B. Repository potential

What can the source-controlled repository now test about the handling of challenged information across time and across institutions?

These planes must never be collapsed.

The controlling rule is:

> **Do not confuse what is presently proved, what is presently under regulatory assessment, and what the repository has newly made capable of proof.**

A regulator's decision may eventually validate, narrow or reject particular propositions. Until then, the repository must not use a complaint as proof of its allegations or use the broader governance theory to bootstrap a GDPR breach.

# 3. HSE / Galway University Hospitals — the live assessed case

The HSE / GUH strand is presently the most procedurally advanced GDPR matter visible in the repository.

On 7 August 2026, the DPC asked for further material concerning:

1. the grounds on which GUH refused Article 16 rectification;
2. the outstanding Article 15 material and evidence supporting its existence;
3. and the method and date by which Article 18 restriction had been invoked.

On 10 August 2026, after receipt of further chronology, the DPC stated that the issues and information concerning HSE / GUH were **under assessment**.

The supplementary primary-evidence submission then placed a set of narrower procedural questions before the Commission, including:

- whether two separate June 2025 Article 16 requests were individually identified and determined;
- whether any Article 12(3) extension was communicated;
- whether the 12 June request for **rectification and annotation** was actually determined when the later refusal introduced it as a request for **erasure**;
- what procedural treatment was given to the express 4 August 2025 Article 18 restriction request;
- what the 5 August Article 15 response meant when it referred both to refusing access and to non-holding;
- and whether the renewed January 2026 Article 16 / Article 18 exercise received a data-protection determination.

The present status is therefore:

> **these questions are live before the DPC; they are not findings of breach.**

# 4. The HSE / GUH Article 18 hinge

This is the clearest contemporary analogue of the repository's broader **What happened?** method.

The documentary sequence is:

> Article 16 accuracy dispute remains unresolved  
> → 4 August 2025 Article 18 restriction expressly invoked, including reference to proposed SIMT processing  
> → **WHAT HAPPENED?**  
> → SIMT later uses the clinical record in its governance review.

The repository presently identifies no intervening controller communication expressly accepting, rejecting, qualifying or otherwise determining the Article 18 request before that review.

That absence must be stated carefully.

It proves a **present documentary gap in the repository**, not that no internal decision was made and not that later processing necessarily infringed Article 18.

The regulatory question now placed before the DPC is exactly the appropriate one:

> **What procedural treatment did the controller give the restriction request, and what effect, if any, was given to it while accuracy remained disputed?**

This is a genuine missing adjudicative bridge capable of being resolved by controller records or regulator inquiry.

# 5. Article 16 — historical accuracy is not the whole question

The HSE / GUH material draws an important distinction between at least three different propositions:

1. **Historical occurrence:** a clinician held and recorded a professional opinion at a particular time.
2. **Historical completeness:** the surviving account may or may not preserve all materially relevant personal data concerning the patient's position, disagreement or later qualifying information.
3. **Present reuse:** an old record may later be processed for a new decisional purpose such as complaint review, governance assessment or current clinical interpretation.

The 12 June 2025 Article 16 request expressly sought **rectification and annotation**.

The 6 September decision introduced the exercise as a request for **erasure**, while later defending retention of historical medical opinion.

The repository should not infer the legal consequence of that discrepancy.

It should preserve the narrower question now before the DPC:

> **Did the controller determine the data right actually exercised, including the completion / annotation aspect, or answer a materially different erasure proposition?**

This is particularly relevant because a proposition can remain historically accurate as a record of what someone thought while still raising separate questions about completeness, qualification or present-tense reuse.

The present status is modest but important:

> **a separate University GDPR case now exists.**

- did University-controlled processing occur in relation to the bedside teaching event?
- what personal data, if any, survive?
- what searches were undertaken?
- what happened to the November 2025 rights exercise?
- if data existed, what happened to the Article 18 restriction request?

# 7. PCRS — 8 / 9 / 10 December 2025 sequence

The PCRS strand is analytically different from the hospital clinical-opinion dispute.

The controller's 8 December position was that the 9 July 2017 date was accurate **“in the context of the LTI scheme”** because it represented the certified application submitted on that date, while the later clinical-confirmation date of 13 July was added for transparency.

The Article 77 complaint sent on 9 December challenged that position under Articles 5(1)(d), 12, 15, 16 and 18 and asked the DPC to determine whether the administrative/scheme context could lawfully sustain the disputed health-data chronology.

A separate provenance event occurred at 08:07 on 9 December: PCRS sent the data subject an email beginning `Hi Miriam` and attached a file expressly named as a **draft letter** to issue to Liam Moloney. Inspection shows the attached draft contains the same substantive PCRS position.

That is useful for document genealogy and version provenance.

It is not evidence of motive.

A generic DPC acknowledgement was issued on 10 December under reference `DPC1225866728`, but because both the PCRS and Medical Council Article 77 complaints were submitted on 9 December and the presently reviewed acknowledgement does not safely identify the controller, this repository does not assign that DPC reference to PCRS without further evidence.

The key PCRS question is therefore not simply:

> Was the date wrong?

It is:

> **What proposition did PCRS say the date represented — clinical diagnosis, scheme certification, eligibility commencement, or something else — and was the stored personal data sufficiently precise about that meaning for the purpose for which it was processed?**

That is a more disciplined accuracy question than assuming clinical and administrative dates must be identical.

# 8. Medical Council — 8 / 9 / 10 December 2025 sequence

The Medical Council strand concerns a different kind of personal data: factual and evaluative propositions embedded in a regulatory decision.

On 8 December 2025, the Council refused Article 16 rectification on the basis that the CEO's decision recorded what was before the decision-maker at the time and that later information did not alter that historical decision. It also refused erasure and restriction and stated that Article 19 did not apply.

The data subject's position was narrower than a general request to reopen a professional-conduct complaint. He alleged that the Council itself held contemporaneous documentary material on 29 July 2025 contradicting the factual premise of non-involvement, and therefore treated the issue as one of accuracy of the Council's own personal data.

On 9 December, he submitted an Article 77 complaint asking the DPC to determine the applicability of Articles 5(1)(d), 16, 17, 18 and 19 to that situation.

As with PCRS, a generic DPC acknowledgement followed on 10 December under `DPC1225866728`, but the present Gmail record does not safely establish which of the two same-day controller complaints received that reference.

The key forensic distinction is:

> **A historical record can accurately record that a decision was made while a separate factual proposition inside that decision may still be disputed as inaccurate.**

Whether GDPR requires any correction, annotation, restriction or other measure is for the DPC / courts, not for this repository to declare.

# 9. The cross-controller pattern the repository can now test

The repository now contains several superficially similar but legally distinct responses to challenged information:

- HSE / GUH: historical clinical opinion and continuity of the medical record;
- PCRS: accuracy **in the context of the scheme**;
- Medical Council: accuracy / preservation of a historical regulatory decision;
- University of Galway: alleged non-response to a request concerning teaching-related data and restriction.

This permits a new research question:

> **When an inherited institutional proposition is challenged, does the controller test the underlying proposition, qualify its meaning, restrict contested use, or preserve the existing record by redefining what the record is said to represent?**

That is a repository-level governance hypothesis.

It is **not yet a finding of systemic GDPR non-compliance**.

The institutions are different. The data purposes are different. The legal bases may be different. The disputed propositions are different. Similar language or outcomes cannot be treated as proof of a single policy or common intent.

What the repository can legitimately do is compare their mechanics under a common source-controlled method.

# 10. The recurring method — EVENT → STATE → TRANSITION → NEXT STATE

Every GDPR strand should now be reconstructed using the same method as the clinical case.

## Event

What rights request, contradictory source, diagnosis, complaint or restriction notice entered the controller's system?

## Pre-existing state

What personal data and institutional proposition existed immediately beforehand?

## What happened?

- Who received the request?
- Who classified it?
- Was it routed to a DPO / Information Governance function?
- What right did the controller understand had been exercised?
- Was processing restricted?
- Was accuracy verified against source material?
- Was an extension issued?
- Was the disputed proposition reformulated rather than corrected?
- Did the contested data continue to be used for a new purpose?
- What decision record should exist?

## Next state

What did the controller communicate or continue processing afterwards?

The most important blank is often the transition itself.

# 12. Actual case status versus repository potential

This distinction should control every future update.

## Actual case status

The formal matters consist of identifiable rights requests, controller responses, Article 77 complaints, DPC acknowledgements and—only for the HSE / GUH matter on the current evidence—an express statement that the issues are **under assessment**.

## Repository potential

The repository can now reconstruct how four institutions handled challenged personal data and compare:

- classification of the right exercised;
- accuracy versus historical-record reasoning;
- completeness / annotation;
- restriction while accuracy was disputed;
- source verification;
- data provenance;
- reuse for later decisional purposes;
- and the presence or absence of visible adjudicative bridges.

The repository's analytical potential is therefore broader than the question any one DPC case may need to decide.

The formal regulatory cases may resolve only narrow GDPR issues.

Counsel may later need the wider longitudinal information-governance reconstruction.

Neither should be used as proof of the other.

---

# 13. What an eventual DPC outcome could change

Each regulator outcome should later be treated as a new evidential event, not merely a victory / defeat label.

Ask:

1. What exact issue did the DPC decide?
2. What did it expressly leave undecided?
3. What controller evidence did it obtain that the repository does not presently hold?
4. Did it identify an internal decision trail for Article 18 or Article 16?
5. Did it distinguish historical accuracy, completeness and present reuse?
6. Did it address source verification?
7. Did it resolve the December 2025 complaint-reference ambiguity?
8. Does the outcome falsify, narrow or strengthen the broader governance model?

A narrow finding should not be inflated into proof of the whole case.

A refusal or no-breach outcome should likewise be analysed for its actual ratio rather than treated as automatic validation of every historical proposition.

# 14. Evidential discipline

Do not state as fact that:

- GUH breached Article 18 because SIMT later processed clinical records;
- Article 16 required historical medical notes to be rewritten;
- University of Galway necessarily holds teaching records;
- PCRS's 9 July date is legally inaccurate merely because clinical confirmation occurred later;
- the Medical Council's regulatory decision was inaccurate merely because contrary evidence is alleged;
- `DPC1225866728` belongs to PCRS or the Medical Council without source evidence resolving that attribution;
- a shared governance mechanism has been proven across controllers;
- or any regulator has endorsed the repository's wider governance theory.

Instead preserve five categories:

### Documentary fact

What the correspondence and records actually show.

### Data-subject allegation

What Liam Moloney asserted in a rights request or Article 77 complaint.

### Controller position

What the relevant institution stated in response.

### Regulatory status

What the DPC has acknowledged, requested, transferred or expressly placed under assessment.

### Repository hypothesis

What the source-controlled comparison makes capable of testing.

# 15. Controlling formulation

The GDPR case is no longer a single dispute over whether old medical records should be changed.

It is a set of source-controlled questions about **what happens to challenged personal data inside institutions**.

For the present:

> **The DPC is actively assessing concrete HSE / GUH Articles 15, 16 and 18 issues; a separate University of Galway complaint was acknowledged this week; PCRS and Medical Council Article 77 complaints were lodged in December 2025; and the repository now has the potential to compare those processes as information-governance systems without treating any complaint as a proven breach.**

The derivative to preserve is:

> **Actual case status tells us what is presently before the regulator. Repository potential tells us what the accumulating sources may eventually allow counsel to prove about the mechanics of challenged information. They are related, but one is not evidence of the other.**

And at every transition, the controlling forensic question remains:

> # **WHAT HAPPENED?**
