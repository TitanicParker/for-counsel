2017–2018

The first treatment-response evidence

The early record does not prove that every foot symptom was neurological. It does show that part of the lower-limb/foot experience changed with neurological medication very early in the course.

A short withdrawal created a natural test

Procyclidine had been used as part of the neurological treatment strategy. In September/October 2017 it was stopped for approximately two weeks because of visual symptoms. The later April 2018 clinical record preserves what happened next: the first thing the patient noticed changing rapidly was the return of the outside-left-foot phenomenon; symptoms worsened somewhat; and he felt more “shifty” and “unrelaxed” off Procyclidine.

The episode is important because it arose from treatment withdrawal rather than from a later theory imposed on the history. It is also limited. The wording is unusual, the source speaks about the outside-left-foot “corn”, and the record does not state that a corn itself was neurologically generated.

An independent contemporaneous account supports the medication sequence

An October 2017 email separately records that the medication had been stopped for around twelve days, restarted at a lower dose and then increased because the lower dose did not “tick off all of the symptoms”. The same email describes marked overall physical improvement and uses the phrase “living in a miracle”.

That email is a separate evidential origin from the later clinic history. It corroborates the withdrawal/reintroduction sequence and the patient’s perception of substantial benefit, but it does not identify every symptom that improved.

The 2018 clinic record preserves both response and treatment caution

By April 2018 the patient reported foot and hand cramps, relief from tension and benefit from toe supports. The clinician recorded that he could be “very much better” if levodopa or another dopamine agonist were used, but that for clearly stated reasons the team did not want to do that “at least for quite some time”.

This is a strong example of why the case should not flatten competing evidence. There was a real treatment-risk strategy. There was also early evidence that the lower-limb/foot experience was not wholly indifferent to neurological medication.

What this evidence establishes

At a minimum, it supports the proposition that at least part of the foot/lower-limb phenomenon demonstrated neurological pharmacological modifiability during the early treatment period.

That proposition is narrower than saying the foot pain was all dystonia, narrower than saying Podiatry was wrong, and narrower than saying levodopa should necessarily have been started immediately.

The question it creates

The expert question is what significance, if any, that early treatment-state evidence should have had for the unresolved 2017 relationship between the foot/lower-limb syndrome and the neurological disorder.

That question becomes more important because it does not disappear. Later records again describe foot sensitivity, lower-limb phenomena judged parkinsonian, OFF-related concerns, medication-timing effects and eventually a marked 2025 levodopa-related change in the morning foot problem.

Evidence boundary. The correct inference is neurological modifiability of at least part of the phenomenon. The sources do not justify a blanket conclusion that every historical foot symptom was dystonia or dopamine-responsive pain.
