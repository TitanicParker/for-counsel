The Case as a Mechanism: From Early Clinical Locking to Eight Years of Inherited Error

Every linked phrase on this page opens the deeper argument, evidence, source, qualification or unresolved question beneath it.

The most important thing I have learned in working through this case is that its difficulty is not primarily medical complexity. Nor is it simply documentary complexity. The difficulty has been one of scale.

At too small a scale, the case dissolves into hundreds of individual facts, qualifications, caveats, and source-status warnings. At too large a scale, it risks becoming a vague allegation of “misdiagnosis” or institutional failure. Neither is adequate. The correct level is the mechanism that connects the individual facts over time.

Seen at that level, the case is not principally about whether severe Parkinsonism was recognised in July 2017. It plainly was. Nor is it about whether PRKN/PARK2 was missed. It plainly was not: it appears expressly within the diagnostic reasoning. Nor is the central proposition that the clinicians had no rationale for delaying levodopa. The record contains one.

The deeper question is what happened when an early Parkinsonian framework became controlling before the patient's full phenotype—particularly the painful bilateral foot and lower-limb problem—had been adequately integrated into it.

My present understanding is that there were two locking events.

The first was clinical and therapeutic.

The second was administrative and documentary.

The consequences of those locks then persisted for approximately eight years.

That is the structure that now seems most capable of explaining the documentary sequence as a whole.

The first lock

The crucial early chronology appears compressed into approximately fourteen hours.

A young man enters hospital with a striking neurological syndrome. Severe Parkinsonism is recognised quickly. That recognition is important and must not be minimised. The clinicians were not imagining disease where none existed. There was profound bradykinesia, rigidity, abnormal gait, symmetry, atypicality, and later objective dopaminergic imaging abnormality.

But recognition of severe Parkinsonism is not the same thing as having understood the full syndrome.

At the point when the treatment framework appears to crystallise, Monaghan had not yet completed the investigation that later appears within the finished record. The painful foot problem had not yet been fully worked through. The relationship between the feet, lower limbs, dystonic-looking gait, burning, mechanical abnormalities, and the Parkinsonian syndrome had not yet been resolved. The developed PRKN discussion that later gives the discharge summary intellectual depth had not yet fully matured.

Yet within roughly fourteen hours, a senior or departmental treatment position appears to have formed around withholding levodopa.

That is the first lock.

The importance of that decision is not merely that levodopa was not prescribed. Its greater importance is that it altered what could thereafter be learned.

If the painful lower-limb and foot phenomenon was in some degree dopaminergically modifiable, then levodopa was not only a potential therapy. It was also a source of information. A response, non-response, partial response, or symptom-specific response might have changed the way the phenotype was understood.

Once levodopa was removed from the immediate clinical repertoire as a settled departmental strategy, that information was not generated.

This is why the distinction between therapeutic levodopa and diagnostic use matters so much. The record clearly explains why the team wished to avoid committing a very young patient to long-term levodopa therapy. It does not equally demonstrate that the separate question—whether dopaminergic responsiveness might clarify part of the unresolved phenotype—was considered as such.

The point is not that a diagnostic levodopa challenge was necessarily required. That is ultimately a clinical question. The point is structural: an early treatment decision may have constrained the acquisition of information that was highly relevant to the unresolved problem.

That creates a feedback loop.

The feet are uncertain.

Levodopa is withheld.

No dopaminergic-response information is generated.

The feet therefore remain uncertain.

Because they remain uncertain and because there are genuine structural foot abnormalities, a podiatry or peripheral explanation becomes increasingly practical.

The alternative pathway then reduces the pressure to reopen the original treatment decision.

Thus the early decision does not merely follow the diagnosis. It begins to shape the diagnosis.

That is what I mean by saying that the emerging phenotype may have been made to fit the situation.

The patient had not yet been fully understood, but the framework in which he was going to be understood was becoming difficult to alter.

The second lock

The second lock is different.

It is not primarily a treatment decision. It is the conversion of a provisional clinical framework into an administratively durable state.

This happens through the combination of diagnosis, LTI, work advice, discharge planning, referral pathways, follow-up, collective language, and the completed discharge artefact itself.

Here the timing matters enormously.

Parkinson's disease became sufficiently definite to govern real-world consequences before all of the diagnostic reasoning visible in the final document had matured.

LTI was processed early.

The patient was organised administratively as somebody with Parkinson's disease.

The feet were sent into a podiatry pathway.

The levodopa strategy had already hardened.

Later investigations continued.

PRKN reasoning developed.

The DaTSCAN was performed after physical discharge.

Its abnormal result was then incorporated into the final clinical document.

None of that requires misconduct. Cumulative medical documentation is normal.

But forensic reading requires us to distinguish the finished artefact from the decision sequence that produced it.

The finished discharge summary creates simultaneity where the underlying history contains sequence.

That matters because the final reader sees all of the following together:

severe Parkinsonism;

a sophisticated young-onset genetic differential;

PRKN/PARK2;

a rationale for delaying levodopa;

podiatry;

LTI;

collective departmental language;

the patient's recorded “good understanding”;

and an abnormal DaTSCAN.

On the page, these elements mutually reinforce one another.

The diagnosis looks considered.

The treatment looks collectively authorised.

The patient looks informed and accepting.

The objective scan appears to confirm the entire structure.

The final record therefore possesses more apparent coherence than the early decision state itself necessarily possessed.

This is the second lock.

The first lock limits the generation of potentially destabilising information.

The second lock takes the resulting state and turns it into a durable institutional history.

That is why the phrase “administrative closure before clinical resolution” has become so useful.

The record itself still contains uncertainty.

It expressly says that the relationship between the leg and foot symptoms and the neurological syndrome was “Not clear.”

That is extraordinary because the same document that preserves uncertainty at the clinical level nevertheless permits certainty to govern administratively.

The feet go to podiatry.

Parkinson's governs neurology.

LTI proceeds.

Treatment strategy proceeds.

Work and follow-up proceed.

The system behaves as though enough has been settled, even while the clinical narrative admits that a central part of the presenting problem remains unresolved.

The best concise description remains:

uncertainty remained clinically while certainty governed administratively.

The protest

The reported protest belongs at the centre of this structure.

It should not be treated as an awkward allegation appended to the documentary case. If it occurred as described, it is the missing counter-record to the locking process itself.

Liam's account is that he protested at the bedside and that four medical students were present.

This matters not merely because it would show that he was unhappy.

It matters because it would show that the patient actively challenged the emerging separation between his neurological condition and his feet at the very point when the pathway was hardening.

That would mean the contemporaneous state was not consensual.

There was a dispute.

There was a competing account of what the feet meant.

There was resistance to the pathway.

And that resistance is not preserved in the completed document.

Instead, what survives is language of collective clinical authority and “good understanding.”

If the protest occurred, the omission does two things at once.

It protects the first lock because no contemporaneous challenge to the levodopa/pathway decision appears in the finished history.

And it protects the second lock because the final document presents an administratively coherent and apparently accepted plan.

This is why I no longer regard the silence of the discharge summary as meaningful evidence against the protest.

The very issue in dispute is whether the discharge artefact adequately preserves the transition at which the protest occurred.

An already-impeached record cannot be permitted simply to prove its own completeness by silence.

The existence of four student witnesses dramatically changes the evidential landscape.

There is now a defined class of potential independent witnesses to the exact hinge.

The proper question is not whether later records mention the protest. The proper question is who those students were and what, if anything, they independently remember.

One credible independent recollection could materially change the status of the entire hinge.

It would transform the protest from a patient account opposed by documentary silence into a contemporaneous event with independent eyewitness support whose omission from the completed patient-facing record would itself demand explanation.

That is why the student question belongs near the top of the evidence-acquisition agenda.

PRKN and the sophistication of the surrounding reasoning

Another reason the case has to be understood carefully is that the clinicians' neurological reasoning was not primitive.

PRKN was not missed.

That is important.

The record identifies a genuinely unusual young-onset Parkinsonian presentation and considers recessive genetic causes including PARK2/PRKN.

The professional context makes this more striking.

Counihan and Dan Healy had published together on Parkin disease before Liam's admission.

Healy had significant Parkinson genetics expertise.

Monaghan sought Healy's input.

The 2017 correspondence contains the phrase that “Myself and Tim” were keen to hold back on levodopa unless Healy advised otherwise.

Thus the case does not resemble a setting in which nobody understood the significance of a young, symmetrical, atypical Parkinsonian syndrome.

The sophisticated reasoning existed.

That actually sharpens the question.

How could sophisticated PRKN reasoning coexist with unresolved dystonic-looking lower-limb phenomenology and severe painful feet without producing a documented integrated synthesis?

This is the phenomenon I would describe as proximity without synthesis.

The relevant elements were near one another:

young onset;

symmetry;

minimal tremor;

dystonic-looking gait;

PRKN;

painful bilateral feet;

burning;

structural abnormalities;

and an expressly uncertain neurological relationship.

Yet they do not appear to have been integrated into a single hypothesis about what part of the foot/lower-limb syndrome might be neurological, what part might be structural or peripheral, and how those possibilities could be tested.

Instead, the case seems to divide into two tracks.

One track is neurologically sophisticated:

young-onset Parkinsonism, genetics, PRKN, Healy, treatment strategy.

The other track is practically simple:

painful feet, corns, burning, structural abnormalities, podiatry.

The unexplained fact is not that either track existed.

It is why they did not adequately converge.

The eight-year life of the lock

The greatest mistake would now be to reduce the case to the first two days of July 2017.

Those days explain how the frame formed.

They do not explain why the case matters.

The significance of the locks lies in what happened afterwards.

Once the discharge architecture existed, later clinicians were not approaching a blank problem.

They inherited a patient whose history already appeared organised.

Parkinson's disease had been established.

The feet had gone to podiatry.

The levodopa strategy had been explained.

The senior department had been invoked.

The patient had recorded “good understanding.”

The DaTSCAN was abnormal.

The document therefore did not simply record history.

It supplied a lens through which later history would be read.

Then the feet kept returning.

Early on, Procyclidine withdrawal and reintroduction produced evidence that at least some component of the symptom complex was neurologically pharmacologically modifiable.

Foot cramps persisted.

Lower-limb abnormalities continued.

Eventually levodopa was introduced.

Later records contain OFF and dystonia reasoning.

Toe curling appears.

The feet remain major.

By 2023 the record itself acknowledges “misalignment” between the clinician's explanatory framework and Liam's understanding of the feet.

That word is immensely revealing.

Years after the original discharge, the same conceptual dispute remains alive.

The issue was never simply resolved and forgotten.

It persisted inside Neurology.

That is the hallmark of path dependency.

An unresolved classification becomes inherited.

New evidence enters.

But instead of beginning from an open question, it is interpreted relative to the inherited classification.

The burden effectively shifts.

The original classification does not have to prove itself again.

New evidence has to become strong enough to dislodge it.

This becomes especially visible in March 2025.

The severe morning foot experience is once again central.

The record says that the feet improve when Parkinson's medication reaches the system.

The neurologist considers additional Sinemet likely to provide further relief.

Sinemet CR is deliberately introduced at night in response to the morning problem, including pain.

The subsequent relief is described as profound.

That is a major event.

It does not prove that every historical foot symptom was dystonic.

It does not erase structural disease, pain modulation, neuropathic possibilities, or the benefit of pregabalin.

But it does demonstrate that the inherited conceptual separation was not capable of explaining the full phenomenon.

At least an important component of the disputed foot experience was substantially modifiable by Parkinsonian treatment.

For Liam, that appears to have been revelatory.

Nine days later he made his formal complaint.

That is why March 2025 is not merely another treatment episode.

It is a rupture in the inherited frame.

For years the record had organised the feet and Parkinson's through a certain distinction.

Then the patient's lived treatment response supplied information that made that distinction unstable.

The complaint followed.

The genetic result

The 2026 Parkin result then adds etiological coherence.

A movement-disorders specialist concluded that Liam's Parkinsonism and dystonia were due to compound heterozygous Parkin mutations.

That result cannot be projected backwards as though it were known in 2017.

But neither can its retrospective significance be artificially minimised.

The same disease family explicitly contemplated in 2017 is eventually confirmed.

Parkinsonism and dystonia, which had appeared as neighbouring but incompletely integrated features in the earlier record, are shown to share a genetic cause.

That does not answer every question about the feet.

It does something more precise.

It confirms that the earlier dystonic phenomenology belonged inside the same underlying neurological disease process as the Parkinsonism.

That makes the historical failure to integrate the painful lower-limb phenotype more important, not less.

Institutional inheritance

The same mechanism may extend beyond clinical care into institutional review.

The later chain appears to run through the historical record, Monaghan's retrospective explanation, the Preliminary Assessment Report, SIMT, and later QPS reliance.

The SIMT conclusion of “no deficit in care” is a real institutional event.

But institutional authority is not the same thing as evidential independence.

If the later review begins from the same 2017 record and the same retrospective treating-clinician account, then authority can accumulate without the disputed hinge ever being independently reconstructed.

That is why the complete PAR and SIMT source architecture matter so much.

Did reviewers obtain the original GP referral?

Did they examine the “Not clear” wording?

Did they ask whether podiatry was adjunctive or effectively dispositive?

Did they reconstruct what was known when levodopa was withheld?

Did they examine the timing of LTI relative to later investigation?

Did they investigate the reported protest?

Did they know four students were present?

Did they consider early pharmacological modifiability?

Did they consider the 2023 misalignment?

Did they consider the deliberate 2025 foot-directed Sinemet intervention and response?

Those are not bureaucratic details.

They determine whether the later “no deficit” conclusion represents fresh independent revalidation or the institutional continuation of an inherited record state.

The latter possibility is especially important because it mirrors the clinical mechanism.

A provisional state acquires authority.

Authority then becomes evidence of correctness.

Subsequent actors inherit both.

The original uncertainty becomes progressively less visible even though no clear independent resolution of it can be demonstrated.

The correct voice

The project has sometimes struggled to say this plainly because it became saturated with the language of caution.

That caution had a legitimate purpose.

It prevented unsupported claims.

It preserved adverse evidence.

It stopped retrospective genetic knowledge being back-projected.

It distinguished patient account from documentary fact.

It prevented institutional repetition from being mistaken for independent corroboration.

Those safeguards must remain.

But safeguards can become distortions when they determine the voice of the entire analysis.

The fact that motive is uncertain does not mean mechanism is uncertain.

The fact that breach requires expert evidence does not mean chronology is uncertain.

The fact that the protest still requires corroboration does not mean its structural significance is uncertain.

The fact that every foot symptom cannot be attributed to dystonia does not mean the neurological-foot relationship remained adequately resolved.

And the fact that Parkinsonism was genuine does not mean the administrative framework built around it was necessarily adequate to the whole patient.

The correct posture is therefore not advocacy and not defensive neutrality.

It is confident reconstruction.

We should say what the evidence strongly suggests at the level at which it strongly suggests it.

The case appears to involve an early Parkinsonian framework that became controlling faster than the full phenotype was understood.

An early treatment decision constrained subsequent investigation.

An administrative/documentary structure then made that provisional state durable.

A reported contemporaneous protest that would have challenged the apparent consensus is absent from the completed artefact, despite four potential student witnesses.

The feet remained unresolved yet were operationally externalised.

The resulting classification persisted.

Later neurological and pharmacological evidence repeatedly destabilised it.

By 2023 the disagreement was explicit.

By 2025 dopaminergic treatment of the foot problem produced profound relief and changed the patient's understanding.

By 2026 PRKN genetically unified Parkinsonism and dystonia.

And later institutional review may itself have inherited the source architecture of the original lock unless the missing PAR and SIMT materials demonstrate otherwise.

That is, in my present understanding, the case.

Its deepest question is not whether Parkinson's disease was a reasonable diagnosis.

It is whether a reasonable provisional diagnosis became an unreasonable permanent organising principle because the system ceased to preserve, generate, or respond adequately to the information capable of changing it.

The most concise formulation I can give is this:

The patient was still being understood when the treatment framework hardened. The record was still developing when the administrative framework hardened. Once both had hardened, later evidence had to fight an institutional history that appeared far more settled than the original clinical problem ever was.
