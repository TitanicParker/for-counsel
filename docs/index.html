Counsel-facing case orientation

The case began with the feet.

Severe Parkinsonism was real. So was the uncertainty about how the painful foot and lower-limb presentation related to it. The case turns on what happened after Parkinson’s disease became the operative framework for care while that relationship remained unresolved.

An open referral became an operative framework

In June 2017 a GP urgently referred a 39-year-old man with an unusual stiff and festinant gait, bilateral foot problems, corns and calluses, impaired movement initiation, increased tone and worsening function. The referral did not announce a settled diagnosis. It asked whether the deterioration was Parkinsonian or reflected another underlying disorder.

Neurology then found severe Parkinsonism. The findings were substantial and later DaTSCAN imaging supported a presynaptic dopaminergic disorder. But the same record also described an extraordinarily abnormal lower-limb presentation, including a footdrop-like posture somewhat suggestive of dystonia, and expressly left the relationship between the leg/foot symptoms and the neurological disorder unresolved: “Not clear.”

Parkinson’s disease nevertheless became the operative diagnosis. Treatment strategy, follow-up, LTI, work advice and patient-facing explanation were organised within that framework, while Podiatry became an operative pathway for the feet.

Why the discharge record matters

The completed discharge artefact is cumulative. The operative discharge and LTI state preceded the formal 13 July DaTSCAN result; the later imaging was then added into the same completed record. That does not imply improper alteration. It does mean the finished document cannot automatically be treated as a single contemporaneous snapshot of everything known and decided at the point of discharge.

The working concept used in this project is administrative crystallization: a clinically plausible diagnosis became sufficiently definite to govern practical decisions while important questions about aetiology and the painful foot/lower-limb phenotype remained inside the narrative. Whether that was clinically appropriate is an expert question, not a conclusion embedded in the label.

The unresolved problem kept returning

Within months, part of the symptom complex involving the outside-left-foot site changed during withdrawal and reintroduction of neurological medication. In later years the feet repeatedly re-entered the neurological record through cramping, sensitivity, OFF phenomena, pain, medication timing and an expressly recorded “misalignment” over how the feet should be understood.

The longitudinal evidence is mixed. Structural foot abnormalities existed. Toe supports and Lyrica helped. Not every medication change produced a clean dopamine-linked effect. The case therefore does not depend on proving that every foot symptom was dystonia. The narrower question is when repeated neurological treatment-state changes should have caused the original unresolved relationship to be reconsidered.

March 2025 changed the evidential position

In March 2025 severe morning foot pain was again central. The record states that the feet felt better once Parkinson’s medication reached the system. The neurologist did not accept that the pain was directly Parkinsonian, but expected that more Sinemet would probably provide further relief and introduced night-time Sinemet CR for the severe morning experience, including pain. The subsequent response was later described as “profound relief currently.”

Nine days after the 10 March clinic, a formal complaint identified the treatment response as a turning point and sought review of the preceding years. That sequence matters both clinically and as contemporaneous evidence of the patient’s knowledge state.

The later genetics did not invent the earlier question

PRKN/PARK2 had already been considered in the earlier clinical architecture. In 2018 the working record described PRKN as what “we/Prof Healy suspect is the cause”. In February 2026 a specialist reported two pathogenic Parkin variants and concluded that the patient’s Parkinsonism and dystonia were due to compound heterozygous Parkin mutations.

The later result does not tell us what clinicians should have concluded in 2017, and it does not convert every historical foot symptom into dystonia. Its narrower significance is that the eventual genetic explanation belonged to a possibility already present in the record.

What this site asks counsel to decide

The central question. What happened when a plausible Parkinsonian diagnosis became the operative framework for care while important questions within the same presentation — particularly aetiology and the relationship of the painful foot/lower-limb syndrome to the neurological disorder — remained unresolved; and when, if at all, should later evidence have caused that unresolved classification to be revisited?

The site does not ask the reader to accept that Parkinsonism was false, that podiatry was necessarily wrong, that levodopa necessarily had to begin in 2017, or that later genetics proves negligence. Those matters are deliberately separated into documentary propositions, expert questions and legal questions.
