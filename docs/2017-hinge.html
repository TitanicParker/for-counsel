2017

The hinge was not failure to recognise Parkinsonism. It was failure to keep an unresolved problem open.

The record already contained severe young-onset Parkinsonism, a dystonic-looking lower-limb abnormality, genuine structural foot disease, and a live PRKN/PARK2 differential. The relationship between the feet and neurological syndrome remained “Not clear.”

1. The case arrived open

The June 2017 GP referral put gait, movement initiation, stiffness, tone, dexterity and bilateral foot problems into one unresolved neurological presentation. The hospital did not receive a settled Parkinson's case with incidental feet.

2. Severe Parkinsonism was recognised quickly

The inpatient examination documented markedly increased symmetrical tone, gross bradykinesia, rigidity and a strikingly abnormal gait. The lower-limb appearance was described as footdrop-like and somewhat suggestive of dystonic posture — expressly “NOT FOOT DROP”. Structural abnormalities including pes planus, hammertoes, corns and calluses were also real.

3. PRKN was already inside the case

The 2017 record expressly considered PRKN/PARK2 because of the very young onset, symmetry, akinetic-rigid pattern, absence of dominant family history and preserved cognition.

Monaghan then supplied Liam and his GP with a Galway departmental genetics article co-authored by Prof Timothy Counihan. The article described Parkin/PRKN disease using features including young onset, dystonia, symmetry and excellent levodopa responsiveness. This was not later literature imported into the case; it formed part of Liam's own discharge package.

4. Levodopa was deliberately deferred

The treatment strategy was to hold levodopa back because Liam was very young and because of concern about dyskinesia, impulse-control disorders and dopamine dysregulation. That caution had a genuine clinical rationale.

The live question is different: what was supposed to trigger reconsideration of that strategy as the phenotype developed?

Controlling question. Given a very young, symmetrical Parkinsonian patient with dystonic-looking lower-limb phenomenology, a live PRKN differential and a knowledge environment in which PRKN was understood to be highly levodopa-responsive, what process required the levodopa-deferral decision to return to an open state?

5. The feet remained “Not clear”

The record considered whether the leg/foot symptoms might be separate, peripheral or neuropathic. But the relationship was not resolved. The record itself says: “Not clear.”

That is the correct starting state. The case does not require a retrospective assertion that every foot symptom was dystonia. It requires examination of what happened to a problem that Neurology itself had not resolved.

6. Tuesday: the treatment authority state

Liam separately recalls a post-Complex-Case-Meeting conversation in which the neurologist said:

“My boss told me not to give you the good stuff. And in time I fear we may come to regret that.”

This is preserved as patient-source evidence. It does not by itself establish who `my boss` meant or what was said inside the meeting. It does, however, belong beside the contemporaneous and later documentary material concerning Counihan, the Weekly Complex Case Meeting and the decision to hold levodopa back.

7. The protest was later — Monday morning before discharge

Liam has now fixed the reported protest to a different event: the Monday morning of his second inpatient week, when he expected to leave hospital the following day and after the medical-student group attached to the ward had changed.

He recalls asking the students at his bedside to summon the duty neurologist. When the neurologist arrived, Liam says he asked whether he really intended to give him a “full NCT and send me out on bald tires”. Liam recalls the neurologist replying: “it's easy for you to get a podiatry appointment as me.”

This account is not merged with the Tuesday Complex Case Meeting evidence. It is a separate patient-source event whose timing, witnesses and relationship to the Podiatry referral can be independently tested.

Patient Statement Log — PSL-0001 and PSL-0003

8. Podiatry became the practical pathway

Podiatry may have been entirely appropriate for genuine structural disease. The forensic question is what exactly was referred while the neurological relationship remained `Not clear`.

The original referral and treatment notes are therefore high-value primary evidence. They should show what information travelled, what Podiatry thought it was treating, what treatment was delivered, whether treatment changed any component of the problem, and whether anything returned to Neurology.

Open the Podiatry evidence framework

9. By 2018 the lock is explicit

The April 2018 Neurology record states that PRKN was what `we/Prof Healy suspect is the cause`. The same note says Liam could “obviously ... be very much better” if given levodopa or another dopamine agonist, while also stating that “we do not want to do that — at least for quite some time.”

By then the issue was no longer whether Parkinsonism was recognised, whether PRKN had been contemplated, or whether dopaminergic treatment might substantially improve him. The treatment remained unavailable because the existing strategy continued.

10. The completed discharge package supplied retrospective coherence

The final 2017 package is unusually extensive. It gathers the admission narrative, examination, diagnosis, genetic reasoning, treatment rationale, senior/external professional context, testing material, scientific literature, administrative consequences and later DaTSCAN material into one explanatory object.

That makes it valuable, but it also means the final document should not be mistaken for a single contemporaneous decision state. The core chronology question remains: what was known when the treatment strategy hardened, and what became known later without causing it to reopen?

Working model. The central problem is not that Parkinsonism or PRKN were missed. It is that a legitimate early levodopa-deferral strategy may have become sufficiently fixed that the unresolved foot/lower-limb problem was never given a reliable reopening mechanism as new information accumulated.
